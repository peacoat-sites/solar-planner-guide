Your electric bill hit $340 last August, and you're done. That's the moment most Texans start seriously thinking about solar, and honestly, it's the right instinct. Texas gets more solar irradiance than almost any other state, ERCOT rates have been volatile enough to make grown adults cry, and the economics can be genuinely compelling. But "Texas is great for solar" is also a sales pitch, and I've watched too many homeowners get burned by a misunderstanding of what the numbers actually look like before they sign anything.

Let's do this properly.

## What Solar Actually Costs in Texas Right Now

| Component | Cost Range | After 30% Federal Credit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 10 kW Solar System | $25,000-$32,000 | $17,500-$22,400 |
| Tesla Powerwall 3 (installed) | $9,000-$11,000 | $6,300-$7,700 |
| Enphase IQ Battery 5P (installed) | $9,000-$11,000 | $6,300-$7,700 |
| Emporia Vue Energy Monitor | ~$80 | ~$80 |

The average residential solar installation in Texas runs between $2.50 and $3.20 per watt before incentives, based on current EnergySage market data. For a typical 10 kW system (which is what a lot of Texas homes need, given the air conditioning load), you're looking at $25,000 to $32,000 before any credits come in.

Then the federal tax credit hits. The Inflation Reduction Act locked in a 30% federal Investment Tax Credit through 2032, so on a $28,000 system, that's $8,400 back when you file your taxes. Important: that's a credit, not a refund. You need actual tax liability to use it fully. I've talked to retirees who were blindsided by this distinction.

Texas has no state income tax, which means [no state solar tax credit](/best-states-for-solar-energy-savings/) to layer on top. That's the bad news. The good news: Texas does have a strong property tax exemption for solar. The added value that solar puts on your home (NREL research puts this at roughly $15,000 for an average system) is exempt from property taxes. In a state where property taxes run 1.5% to 2.5% annually, that exemption quietly saves you $300 to $600 every year for the life of the system.

Most Texas utilities also offer a sales tax exemption on solar equipment. Not all installers will mention this automatically, so ask.

After the federal credit, net system cost lands around $17,500 to $22,500 for most homeowners. Payback periods typically run 7 to 11 years, depending on your usage, your specific utility, and whether you add battery storage.

## The Grid Situation You Actually Need to Understand

Here's what most people don't realize when they shop for solar in Texas: which grid you're on matters enormously, and it shapes almost every financial calculation.

About 90% of Texans are on ERCOT, the state's independent grid. ERCOT operates under deregulated retail energy markets in most major metros (Houston, Dallas, Austin, San Antonio area). That means you can shop for your electricity provider, which is genuinely useful for solar owners trying to find plans with good buyback rates for the power their panels send back to the grid.

Net metering, the system where utilities give you dollar-for-dollar credit for every kilowatt-hour you export, doesn't exist in Texas the way it does in California or New Jersey. No Texas utility is required by law to offer it. What most Texas solar owners get instead is an avoided-cost buyback rate, often somewhere between $0.03 and $0.08 per kWh. Meanwhile you're buying power at $0.12 to $0.16 per kWh (or higher during peak demand). That gap matters a lot.

The practical implication: solar saves you the most money in Texas when you consume the power yourself, not when you export it. Running your dishwasher, charging your car, or pre-cooling your house during peak solar hours (10am to 3pm) makes your system dramatically more effective than just letting surplus power flow to the grid for a few cents.

Some retail electric providers in Texas have created solar-friendly buyback plans specifically to attract this customer segment. Green Mountain Energy, Rhythm Energy, and Reliant have all offered versions of these. Rates change constantly, so compare them on PowerToChoose.org before you commit to an installer, because your plan selection will affect your ROI as much as which panels you pick.

## Battery Storage: Overkill or Necessity?

February 2021 settled this debate for a lot of people.

Without a battery, a grid-tied solar system goes dark during an outage. This is a safety requirement (to protect line workers), and it's a fact that more than a few solar salespeople gloss over. During Winter Storm Uri, solar owners without batteries had panels sitting on their roofs doing nothing while the grid was down. Hard lesson.

A Tesla Powerwall 3 currently retails around $9,000 to $11,000 installed. An Enphase IQ Battery 5P runs comparable numbers. These costs also qualify for the 30% federal tax credit when installed alongside solar (or even on an existing solar system). So the after-credit cost is more like $6,300 to $7,700.

Is it worth it? Depends on where you live and [how risk-tolerant you are](/solar-panels-in-california-nem-30/). Coastal Texas and areas with aging infrastructure see more outages, and a battery there feels less like a luxury. In parts of North Texas with newer infrastructure, you might go a decade without a meaningful outage. I wouldn't tell everyone to buy one, but if your household has someone on medical equipment or you simply can't handle another week without power, the math is secondary to the peace of mind.

One underrated option: a hybrid inverter like the SMA Sunny Boy Storage or the Enphase IQ8 series can give you limited backup from your panels during daytime outages even without a large battery bank. The Enphase IQ8 microinverters specifically have a "Sunlight Backup" feature that a lot of homeowners don't know about. Worth asking your installer if that's built into their proposal.

For real-time monitoring of what your system is producing and consuming, the Emporia Vue clips onto your breaker panel, costs around $80, and shows you exactly where your power is going. That data changes behavior in ways that improve your solar ROI surprisingly fast.

## Choosing an Installer in Texas: The Part Nobody Writes About

Texas has no statewide licensing requirement specifically for solar installation. Let that sink in. Any contractor with an electrician's license can legally install solar panels in Texas. That doesn't mean most do it badly, but it means you can't shortcut your vetting process the way you might in California or Massachusetts, where stricter certification requirements have raised the floor.

What to actually check:

Look for NABCEP (North American Board of Certified Energy Practitioners) certification on the installer's team. It's the gold standard, and it's not easy to get. Ask specifically which employees are certified, not just whether the company is affiliated with someone who is.

Get at least three quotes. EnergySage data consistently shows homeowners who compare multiple quotes save an average of 20% on their final system cost. In Texas, I've seen quotes for identical system sizes come in $6,000 to $9,000 apart. That spread is real.

Ask for a production estimate in kWh per year, not just system size in watts. A well-designed 9 kW system will beat a poorly designed 11 kW system. Shading, roof angle, azimuth (compass direction), and panel tilt all affect output significantly. Ask to see the software model (PVWatts or Aurora Solar are common) and what assumptions they used.

Watch for long-term monitoring contracts and lease agreements. Solar leases were heavily pushed in Texas about six years ago and they've caused real headaches: homeowners who want to sell their house discovering that the prospective buyer has to assume a 20-year lease obligation. If an installer is strongly pushing a lease or PPA (power purchase agreement), make sure you understand exactly what you're signing and what your exit costs are.

And check reviews specifically on Google and the Better Business Bureau for the local Texas branch, not just the national brand. Some large national installers have excellent national reputations but mediocre regional operations.

## How Long Before You Break Even?

For a $28,000 system with the federal tax credit applied, net cost of $19,600, producing 13,000 kWh per year in central Texas, and with an average retail rate of $0.14 per kWh: you're looking at about $1,820 in annual savings if you're self-consuming most of that production. Payback period: roughly 10.8 years.

Push your self-consumption higher (time your laundry, pool pump, EV charging to daytime hours) and that payback tightens. Rates go up, as they have pretty much every year in Texas, and it tightens further. Panels degrade slowly, about 0.5% per year on modern equipment, so you'll still have a productive system at year 25.

The U.S. Department of Energy's homeowner solar guide shows 25-year lifetime savings for Texas systems in the $25,000 to $40,000 range, depending heavily on local rates and usage. Those numbers are real, but they require the system to be well-designed and installed, not just adequate.
