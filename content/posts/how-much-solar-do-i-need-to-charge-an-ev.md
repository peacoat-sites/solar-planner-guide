Sizing solar for an EV is simpler than most installers make it sound. You need roughly 2 to 4 additional kilowatts of panel capacity to cover average American driving, and the exact number depends on three things: how many miles you drive annually, how efficient your car is, and how much sun your roof actually gets.

That's the short version. The long version matters, because getting this wrong in either direction costs real money. Undersize the array and you're still buying grid electricity for most of your charging. Oversize it and you've paid for panels that produce more than your utility will credit.

A quick note on federal incentives: the 30% Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) expired for systems installed after December 31, 2025 under Public Law 119-21. If you're buying solar today in 2026, there's no federal tax credit to subtract from your cost math. Some states still offer their own incentives, so check your state's energy office before assuming you're getting nothing back.

## The Core Calculation

Start with your miles. The average American drives about 13,500 miles per year, according to the Federal Highway Administration. Your EV's [efficiency rating](/solar-panel-efficiency-ratings-explained/) tells you how many kilowatt-hours it consumes per mile. Most mainstream EVs today land between 3.0 and 4.5 miles per kWh, though that number drops in cold weather, at highway speeds, and when you're running heat.

Divide your annual miles by your car's efficiency to get your annual charging demand in kWh. Then divide that by your location's annual peak sun hours (which you'll find in NREL's PVWatts calculator, available free at pvwatts.nrel.gov) to get a rough panel capacity in kilowatts.

**Example:** Say you drive 12,000 miles per year in a vehicle that averages 3.5 miles per kWh. That's 3,429 kWh of annual charging demand. Your roof in Phoenix gets about 5.5 peak sun hours per day, so over 365 days you'd divide 3,429 by 2,007.5 (5.5 x 365). That gives you roughly 1.7 kW of panel capacity dedicated to EV charging. Add your household electricity use on top of that, and you're looking at a combined system in the 6 to 9 kW range for a typical home.

**Example:** The same driver in Seattle, where peak sun hours average closer to 3.5 per day, needs 3,429 / 1,277.5 = about 2.7 kW just for the car. Same miles, same car, 60% more panel capacity required.

## Why "Peak Sun Hours" Isn't What You Think

This trips up a lot of people. Peak sun hours aren't the number of daylight hours in your city. They're a measure of solar irradiance: specifically, how many hours per day your location receives the equivalent of 1,000 watts of sunlight per square meter. A partly cloudy location might have 12 hours of daylight but only 3.5 peak sun hours.

NREL's data shows this varies from about 2.9 in Anchorage to 6.5 in Phoenix, with most of the lower 48 falling between 3.5 and 5.5. The practical effect is significant: a homeowner in Boston needs roughly 40% more panel capacity than one in Albuquerque to generate the same annual kWh. Installers sometimes gloss over this when they're quoting you a system size, partly because bigger systems mean bigger sales. Run the NREL PVWatts numbers yourself and you won't be surprised.

Roof orientation matters too. South-facing arrays at a tilt matching your latitude perform best. A west-facing roof typically loses 10 to 20% of production compared to true south, east-facing even more so. If your best roof exposure isn't south, adjust your panel sizing upward accordingly.

## Does Your Charger Setup Actually Matter?

Yes, more than most people realize. A standard 120V Level 1 outlet adds roughly 3 to 5 miles of range per hour. That's fine for plug-in hybrids but painfully slow for a full EV with 60 to 100+ kWh of battery capacity. A Level 2 charger running on 240V at 7.2 kW adds 20 to 30 miles per hour, and it integrates far more cleanly with solar.

The reason timing matters: most solar production happens between 10 AM and 3 PM. If your car is parked at home during those hours and you have a smart Level 2 charger that lets you schedule charging to coincide with solar production, you can directly use solar electrons rather than exporting them and importing grid power overnight. Chargers like the [Emporia Smart EV Charger](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=emporia+smart+ev+charger&tag=contentportfo-20) *(note: this is an affiliate link, and the site may earn a commission on purchases)* offer solar-sync scheduling features that let you match charging to your production window without a full home energy management system.

If you charge primarily overnight, you're almost certainly charging on grid power regardless of your solar array's size, because solar doesn't produce at night and batteries large enough to store a full day's production add significant cost.

## Battery Storage: When It's Worth Adding

Adding a [home battery](/best-home-battery-2026/) like the Tesla Powerwall 3 or Enphase IQ Battery 5P changes the equation. You can store afternoon solar production and release it for overnight charging. That closes the timing gap, but it comes at a cost: home batteries run $10,000 to $18,000 installed before any state incentives, according to EnergySage's market data.

Whether the math works depends on your utility's [net metering policy](/net-metering-policy-by-state/). If your utility credits solar exports at close to retail rate, adding a battery primarily for EV charging rarely pays off on a strict financial basis. You'd essentially be paying $10,000+ to avoid buying grid electricity that might cost you $500 to $800 per year at typical EV charging rates. The payback there is brutal.

Where batteries make financial sense is under time-of-use rate structures with high peak pricing (think California's TOU rates, where on-peak power can run 3 to 4 times the off-peak rate), or in areas with poor net metering that credit exports at wholesale rather than retail. In those cases, storing solar and using it during peak hours reduces your bill enough to justify the investment. Check your utility's rate schedule before assuming.

You can get a rough picture of your combined solar-plus-EV savings with this [solar savings calculator](/solar-savings-calculator/), which lets you adjust for your location and consumption.

## When Solar for EV Charging Is a Poor Fit

Worth saying plainly: solar doesn't make financial sense for everyone adding an EV. A few situations where the numbers usually don't work:

If you're in a rented property or a condo, rooftop solar isn't your call to make. Community solar subscriptions exist in some states, but the economics are softer.

If your roof is heavily shaded by trees or neighboring buildings and you're not willing to remove the obstruction, you'll produce far less than any calculator assumes. NREL's shading analysis tools can quantify this, but many homeowners skip that step.

If you drive very low mileage, say under 7,000 miles a year, the incremental solar you'd need for the car is so small (under 1.5 kW) that bundling it into a system purchase barely affects the size calculation. It's not a reason to skip solar, but it's also not a compelling argument to go solar just for the car.

And if your utility has gutted net metering and doesn't credit solar exports at a reasonable rate, your overall payback period stretches considerably. California's NEM 3.0 structure, which dramatically reduced export credits starting in 2023, shifted the math significantly toward self-consumption rather than grid export strategies.

### FAQ

#### How many solar panels does it take to charge an electric car?

It depends on your driving habits and location, but most EV owners need 4 to 10 additional panels (typically 400W each) to cover their driving miles. High-efficiency EVs like the Ioniq 6 at the low end, trucks like the Lightning at the high end. Run your own numbers using NREL's PVWatts and your car's EPA efficiency rating.

#### Can I charge an EV entirely with solar panels?

Yes, over the course of a year you can produce enough solar electricity to offset all of your EV's charging demand. You won't be charging directly from panels in real time for every session, but if your system is sized correctly, the annual kWh produced will roughly equal the kWh consumed for driving.

#### Is a solar battery required to charge an EV from solar at night?

You'd need a battery to use solar power after dark, since panels produce nothing at night. Without storage, overnight charging draws from the grid. Many owners find the financial case for adding a battery just for overnight EV charging is weak unless they're on a steep time-of-use rate structure.

#### Does adding an EV change how big my whole solar system should be?

Yes, often significantly. An EV can add 2,000 to 6,000 kWh of annual electricity demand depending on mileage and efficiency. That's the equivalent of adding a small second home's worth of electrical load. If you're designing a new system, calculate your combined home-plus-EV demand before deciding on system size.

#### Is there still a federal tax credit for solar installed in 2026?

No. The 30% Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) expired for homeowner installations completed after December 31, 2025. Systems installed in 2026 or later do not qualify. Some states and utilities offer their own incentives; check your state energy office for current programs.
