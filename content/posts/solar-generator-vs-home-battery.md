Solar generators and home batteries solve different problems. If you need something you can grab and run during a camping trip or a brief outage, a solar generator makes sense. If you want to keep your refrigerator, medical devices, and lights running through a multi-day grid failure, or if you're trying to cut your electricity bill with stored solar energy, a home battery is what you actually need. The confusion between them is real and understandable, because marketing has blurred the lines significantly.

A solar generator is essentially a portable power station with a built-in battery, an inverter, and sometimes a folding solar panel included in the bundle. The EcoFlow DELTA Pro and Jackery Explorer 2000 Pro are two of the best-known examples. They're self-contained, they weigh somewhere between 30 and 100 pounds, and they're not wired into your home. A home battery, by contrast, is a fixed, wall-mounted system like the [Tesla Powerwall](/tesla-powerwall-cost/) 3 or Enphase IQ Battery 5P. It connects to your home's electrical panel, charges from your rooftop solar array or the grid, and can power whole circuits or even your entire house automatically when the power goes out.

## What You're Actually Comparing

The core difference isn't really about solar panels. It's about how much energy you can store, how fast you can discharge it, and how the system integrates with your home.

| Feature | Solar Generator | Home Battery |
|---|---|---|
| Typical usable capacity | 0.5 – 3.6 kWh | 5 – 20+ kWh |
| Continuous output | 1,000 – 3,600W | 3,840 – 11,500W |
| Installation | None required | Professional, permitted |
| Grid integration | No | Yes |
| Automatic transfer (whole-home) | No | Yes (most systems) |
| Federal 30% ITC eligible | No (generally) | Yes |
| Typical all-in cost | $500 – $3,500 | $10,000 – $18,000 installed |
| Portability | Yes | No |
| Lifespan | 500 – 1,000 cycles | 3,000 – 6,000+ cycles |

That cycle life gap matters more than most buyers realize. A portable unit with 500 cycles that you charge and drain twice a week hits the end of its useful life in roughly five years. A Powerwall 3, rated for unlimited cycles with 10 years of warranty coverage, is a fundamentally different product category.

## What a Solar Generator Can and Can't Do

For what it's designed for, a solar generator is genuinely good. It'll run a CPAP machine, charge phones and laptops, power a box fan or a small mini-fridge, and maybe keep a lamp on. EcoFlow's DELTA Pro, one of the larger portable units, holds 3.6 kWh and can output 3,600W continuously, which is enough to run a mid-size window AC unit for a few hours.

What it can't do: run a central air system, an electric water heater, or an electric range. It can't power your whole panel. It won't switch on automatically when the grid drops. And recharging it from folding panels is slow. Most portable panels run 100 to 400 watts. Recharging a 2 kWh unit from a 200W panel takes around 10 hours of direct sun, assuming minimal losses. That's the kind of math that bites people when they're in day two of a grid outage.

**Example:** Assume a household needs to run a refrigerator (150W average), a few LED lights (50W total), and phone chargers (30W) during an outage. Total draw: roughly 230W. A 2 kWh solar generator at 80% usable capacity provides about 1.6 kWh. At 230W, that's roughly 7 hours of runtime. Add a 200W solar panel in a region averaging 4 peak sun hours, and you're adding about 0.8 kWh per day, which covers about half the daily demand. You'd be managing the gap manually, running the generator for priority loads only.

## The Case for a Home Battery

EnergySage's market data shows that homeowners with solar panels who add a battery are storing excess midday generation and drawing it back during evening peak rate hours, which in time-of-use utility markets can be a meaningful monthly reduction on the electricity bill. That's something no portable generator can do.

The economics do require real scrutiny though. A Powerwall 3 installed runs roughly $12,000 to $15,000 before incentives. After the federal 30% Investment Tax Credit (ITC), which applies to battery systems charged primarily by solar, you're looking at $8,400 to $10,500. Add state incentives where available (California's SGIP, for example, pays rebates per watt-hour of storage for eligible households) and the gap narrows further.

The Solar Energy Industries Association (SEIA) reports that residential battery attachment rates have increased sharply in recent years, driven partly by grid reliability concerns and partly by time-of-use billing becoming more common. That trend is real. So is the reality that in a sunny state without frequent outages and no time-of-use rates, the financial case alone may take 10 to 15 years to pencil out.

## Who Should Buy Which

Decision rule: if you don't have rooftop solar and you want backup power for occasional, short outages, buy a solar generator. If you have rooftop solar (or plan to install it), experience outages longer than 12 hours, or pay time-of-use electricity rates, a home battery earns its cost.

There's also a middle path that gets overlooked. Some homeowners use a portable power station as a first layer of backup while they evaluate whether a full battery installation makes financial sense. The EcoFlow DELTA Pro even has an add-on battery module that expands capacity to 7.2 kWh, which starts to overlap with the low end of fixed systems. It won't integrate with your panel, but it gives you substantially more runway.

One group that should not default to a home battery: renters, people planning to move in the next two to three years, and anyone whose roof isn't solar-ready yet. The payback period on a fixed battery installation depends on you being there to realize the savings.

## A Word on Capacity Claims

Both product categories inflate their numbers in marketing. "2000Wh" sounds like 2 kWh of usable storage, but most manufacturers derate to 80 to 90% to protect battery health. A unit advertised at 2,048Wh might deliver 1,640Wh in practice. Home batteries are more transparent about this: Enphase publishes usable capacity separately from total capacity in its spec sheets. For portable units, look for the usable capacity figure in the technical specs, not the headline number.

Also, peak vs. continuous output is a real distinction. A unit that claims "3,600W peak" might sustain only 1,800W continuously. Running a sump pump or refrigerator compressor, both of which have surge draws at startup, can trip a unit that's undersized on continuous output.
