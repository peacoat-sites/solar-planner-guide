Most solar sellers leave money on the table. Not because they priced the system wrong, but because they didn't understand what they were actually selling.

Here's what the typical "going solar" article skips entirely: the moment you decide to sell your house, your solar panels stop being just an energy tool and become a negotiating variable. Whether they help or hurt you depends almost entirely on one thing you probably glossed over when you signed your installation contract.

## Owned vs. Leased: This Is the Whole Ballgame

| Ownership Type | Transfer at Sale | Warranty Coverage | Key Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Owned Outright | Automatic, mostly paperwork | 10-year workmanship; 25-year manufacturer panels; inverter varies | None; adds ~$4/watt to sale price |
| Solar Loan (Paid Off) | Automatic, mostly paperwork | 10-year workmanship; 25-year manufacturer panels; inverter varies | None; adds ~$4/watt to sale price |
| Lease (20-25 years) | Requires buyer approval & credit check | Solar company retains ownership | Buyer's lender may refuse; 30-60 day delay; buyout costs $10,000-$30,000 |
| Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) | Requires buyer approval & credit check | Solar company retains ownership | Buyer's lender may refuse; 30-60 day delay; buyout costs $10,000-$30,000 |

If you own your panels outright, or you've paid off a solar loan, congratulations. Your panels are real property, they transfer with the house, and according to a widely-cited Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory study, they add roughly $4 per watt of installed capacity to your home's sale price. On a 7-kilowatt system, that's about $28,000 in added value. Buyers don't always pay full freight, and local markets vary enormously, but the direction is clearly positive.

If you leased your panels, or you're still paying a power purchase agreement (PPA), you don't own them. The [solar company does](/sunrun-vs-sunpower-vs-tesla-solar/). And what you're really selling is a contract obligation, not an asset. That distinction has killed more home sales than most agents will admit to you.

I've talked to homeowners who were genuinely blindsided by this. They'd been happily paying $85 a month to SunRun or Sunnova for years, their electricity bills were low, life was good. Then they listed the house and their agent called with bad news: the buyer's lender won't approve the loan because the leased panels complicate the title.

Leases typically run 20-25 years. When you sell, you have three options: transfer the lease to the buyer (requires their approval and a credit check by the solar company), buy out the lease before closing (can run $10,000 to $30,000 depending on remaining term), or ask the company to remove the panels (rare, and they usually won't). None of these are fast. Budget 30-60 extra days minimum if you're dealing with a lease transfer.

The [Solar Energy Industries Association (SEIA)](https://www.seia.org/) tracks leasing rates, and while the share of lease/PPA deals has dropped significantly since the mid-2010s, there are still millions of homes with leased systems out there. If you bought before 2018, check your paperwork before you list.

## What Actually Transfers at Closing

For owned systems, the transfer is mostly paperwork, but there's more of it than you'd expect.

Your installer's workmanship warranty, typically 10 years, usually transfers automatically. Same with manufacturer warranties on the panels themselves. Canadian Solar, Qcells, and most major manufacturers offer 25-year product warranties that are tied to the equipment, not the owner, so buyers should be covered.

The inverter is the piece most sellers forget to mention. String inverters from brands like SMA or Fronius often carry 10-year warranties that can be extended. Microinverters from Enphase come with 25-year warranties that are explicitly transferable. Worth putting this in your disclosure packet.

Net metering agreements are different. These are contracts with your utility, and they don't always transfer cleanly. Some utilities will roll the new owner into the same arrangement. Others treat it as a new application, which in states that have grandfathered older, more favorable net metering rates (looking at you, California NEM 2.0 versus NEM 3.0), could mean the buyer gets materially worse economics than you had. Disclose this. Buyers' agents are starting to ask about it specifically, and if you don't bring it up and it comes out later, it looks like you were hiding something.

Home energy monitors give buyers visibility into what the system is producing. The [Emporia Vue Energy Monitor](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Emporia+Vue+Energy+Monitor&tag=contentportfo-20) (about $70 on Amazon) clips onto your breaker panel and gives real-time production and consumption data. Leave one installed, or include it in the sale. It's a small gesture that signals you've maintained the system. *(Note: This site may earn a commission on purchases.)*

## How Much Do Solar Panels Actually Add to Your Sale Price?

It depends. Anyone who gives you a precise number without knowing your market is guessing.

The $4-per-watt figure comes from research on markets like California, New Jersey, and Massachusetts, where solar is common, buyers understand it, and appraisers have enough comparable sales to work with. In those markets, a 7-10 kW owned system can meaningfully move your sale price.

In markets where solar is still unusual, things get murkier. Appraisers in rural Texas or suburban Ohio may not have the comps to formally credit the system. That doesn't mean buyers won't value it, but it might not show up in the appraised value, which matters for financed purchases. I've seen sellers in thin solar markets get almost nothing added to their appraisal even with a spotless, fully-owned 8 kW system installed two years prior.

Age matters. A 12-year-old system with first-generation panels and an [aging string inverter](/solar-installation-process-step-by-step/) is not worth the same as a two-year-old setup with high-efficiency panels and Enphase microinverters. Panels degrade at roughly 0.5% per year according to NREL data, so a 15-year-old system is producing maybe 7-8% less than it was at installation. That's manageable, but it's real, and a savvy buyer will factor it in.

Get a solar-specific appraisal addendum if you can. The Appraisal Institute has guidelines for valuing PV systems (the "Income Approach" and "Cost Approach" methods), and some appraisers will use them if you ask. Your listing agent may not know to request this, so you might have to push.

## Before You List: A Pre-Sale Solar Checklist

Pull your original installation contract and identify whether you own, lease, or have a PPA. If you're unsure, [your installer](/how-to-choose-a-solar-installer/) or a quick search of your county's property records (look for a UCC-1 financing statement filed by the solar company) will tell you.

Get the system inspected. A $150-250 inspection from a certified solar technician will catch any underperforming panels, degraded connections, or inverter issues before a buyer's inspector does. Fixing a $400 inverter issue proactively is a lot cheaper than a price reduction during negotiations.

Gather all your monitoring data. Most modern systems log production through apps like SolarEdge, Enphase Enlighten, or SMA's Sunny Portal. Export 12 months of production data. Showing a buyer "this system produced 10,847 kWh last year, which offset $1,850 in electricity costs at our current rate" is far more persuasive than saying "it saves money."

Locate your warranties, permits, and the interconnection agreement with your utility. These belong in your disclosure packet. Missing permits are a legitimate red flag and can delay or derail a closing.

If you have a solar loan through a lender like Mosaic or GreenSky, get a payoff quote early. Some solar loans, like PACE financing (Property Assessed Clean Energy), attach to the property and will transfer to the buyer whether they want them or not. That needs to be disclosed clearly upfront, or you'll have a very unhappy buyer at the closing table.

## What Buyers Are Actually Thinking

A reader emailed me last year after inheriting a home with a 9-year-old leased SunPower system. She didn't want the panels, didn't want the lease, and couldn't afford the buyout. The sale dragged on for four months while they worked through a lease transfer to a buyer who was willing to take it on. The seller dropped the price $8,000 to make the deal work.

That's an extreme case, but it shows what buyers weigh: future savings versus hassle, obligation, and uncertainty. Most buyers are fine taking on a well-maintained, fully-owned system in a state with good net metering. They get lower electric bills from day one. That's an easy sell.

The deal gets harder when you add a lease with 15 years left, a solar loan the buyer has to refinance around, or a system old enough that major components will need replacement soon. Buyers in competitive markets might just pass. Buyers who are interested will negotiate hard on price.

Pull out your solar contract and read the ownership and transfer clauses right now. Everything else flows from that one document. If you're leasing and you're even thinking about selling in the next few years, get a buyout quote today. Rates and remaining balances shift, and knowing your number early gives you options that waiting will take away.
