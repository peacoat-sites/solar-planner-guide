SunPower, LG, and Panasonic have long been the three names that show up whenever a homeowner wants the best residential [solar panels money](/zero-down-solar-financing-options/) can buy. But the competitive picture has shifted significantly, and buying decisions made on five-year-old comparisons can lead you somewhere you didn't intend to go.

The short version: SunPower is still making panels and is arguably the strongest brand standing, though it went through bankruptcy restructuring in 2023 and emerged as a more focused company. LG exited the [solar panel](/solar-panel-shading-impact-on-output/) business entirely in 2022. Panasonic stopped manufacturing its own HIT panels and wound down its solar module production around the same time. So if you're shopping today, one of these three is a live option and two are essentially legacy products you'd only encounter as used inventory or on homes being resold.

That matters a lot before you read any spec sheet.

## What Happened to LG and Panasonic

LG's exit from solar was not a performance story. Their NeON 2 and NeON R panels were genuinely excellent, with [efficiency ratings](/solar-panel-efficiency-ratings-explained/) in the 21-22% range and strong temperature coefficients. The company left because solar panels were a small and unprofitable business unit relative to LG's other operations. They announced the exit in February 2022 and fulfilled existing warranty obligations for a period, but the practical reality for a new buyer today is that there's no manufacturer standing behind a warranty claim. That's disqualifying for a 25-year asset.

Panasonic's situation is similar in outcome, different in structure. Their HIT (Heterojunction Intrinsic Thin-layer) technology was genuinely innovative, and the EverVolt panels they sold into 2022 were competitive with anything on the market. Panasonic licensed the HIT technology and manufacturing know-how to other companies, and some of that IP lives on in the heterojunction panels sold under other brands. But the Panasonic-branded modules are gone.

If you see a quote that includes LG or Panasonic panels from an installer today, ask hard questions. Where are those panels coming from? Are they new old stock? How is the warranty backstopped? The right answer might not exist.

## SunPower Today

SunPower's bankruptcy filing in 2023 was alarming news, but the outcome matters more than the filing. The company restructured, sold off its installer network (which became a separate entity), and refocused on its core panel manufacturing and dealer channel. The Maxeon cell technology, which was already its own publicly traded company (Maxeon Solar Technologies), supplies the high-efficiency cells at the core of SunPower's residential panels.

What you're actually getting with a SunPower panel is a back-contact cell design that puts all the electrical contacts on the rear surface. This eliminates shading losses from front-side busbars, which is part of why the efficiency numbers are so high. The SunPower Maxeon 6 panels currently list efficiency around 22.8%, and the temperature coefficient is typically around -0.27%/°C, which is meaningfully better than conventional monocrystalline silicon at roughly -0.35 to -0.40%/°C. That gap matters most if you live somewhere hot.

SunPower's warranty is also unusually strong: a 25-year product and power warranty that covers both defects and performance degradation, with a floor of 92% output retained at year 25. Most commodity panels guarantee around 80-84% at 25 years.

The honest downside: SunPower panels are expensive, often 20-30% higher per watt than mainstream monocrystalline panels from Qcells or Canadian Solar. And the company's financial history is now a real consideration. Buying a panel with a 25-year warranty from a company that just restructured involves some leap of faith. That said, Maxeon Solar Technologies as a separate entity provides some protection for the technology and cell supply chain.

## Who Should Actually Buy SunPower

Premium efficiency panels make the most financial sense in a specific set of circumstances, not universally. If you have a small roof and need to maximize output per square foot, the efficiency premium pays off in kilowatt-hours you couldn't otherwise generate. Same logic applies if you're in a hot climate where the better temperature coefficient produces meaningfully more output on summer afternoons.

**Example:** Assume a 400 sq ft of usable south-facing roof space. At 22.8% efficiency, SunPower panels can fit roughly 7.6 kW into that footprint. A standard 20% efficient panel from the same space yields about 6.7 kW. That's nearly 1 kW of additional capacity, which at a typical U.S. household consumption and solar irradiance level (assume 1,400 kWh/kW/year from NREL's PVWatts for a mid-Atlantic location) generates about 1,400 additional kWh annually. At $0.17/kWh average retail rate, that's roughly $238/year in extra production. Whether that justifies a $1,500-2,000 premium on the panel cost alone depends on your time horizon and financing assumptions, but the math is real and checkable.

If you have ample roof space and are installing in a mild climate, the case for paying the SunPower premium weakens. A well-made 410W Qcells or Canadian Solar panel at $2.50-$3.00/watt installed will give you essentially the same energy output per dollar spent over 25 years.

The [Solar Energy Industries Association (SEIA)](https://www.seia.org/) tracks market data showing that conventional monocrystalline panels now dominate residential installations by volume precisely because they've closed the efficiency gap at a much lower price point. Premium panels are a smart choice in the right situation, not a default win.

## The Federal Tax Credit and Panel Choice

One thing that changes the math: the federal residential clean energy credit currently allows you to claim 30% of your total installed system cost. That includes the panels, inverter, racking, and labor. Because the credit applies to total cost, buying more expensive panels means a larger absolute credit. If your SunPower system costs $28,000 and a standard panel system costs $22,000, your credit is $8,400 vs. $6,600. The gap in out-of-pocket cost narrows from $6,000 to $4,200. The [U.S. Department of Energy's homeowner solar guide](https://www.energy.gov/eere/solar/homeowners-guide-going-solar) has good background on how to claim the credit using IRS Form 5695.

## Monitoring and Keeping an Eye on Performance

Regardless of which panels you end up with, real-time monitoring is how you catch a problem before it costs you months of lost production. SunPower's mySunPower app is integrated with their systems, but third-party monitors can work across any inverter brand. A device like the [Emporia Vue whole-home energy monitor](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Emporia+Vue+energy+monitor) (the site may earn a commission on purchases) lets you track solar production against consumption independently of your installer's app, which is useful if you ever have a warranty dispute.
