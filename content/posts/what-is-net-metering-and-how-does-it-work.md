Most solar shoppers spend weeks obsessing over panel brands and installation quotes, and then barely glance at the three words that will probably determine whether their system actually pays off: net metering policy.

I'll be honest: when I first started explaining solar economics to homeowners, I undersold this. I'd mention net metering in passing, like a footnote. That was a mistake. Because depending on where you live, the difference between a strong net metering policy and a weak one can easily shift your 25-year return by $15,000 to $30,000. Same panels. Same roof. Completely different financial outcome.

So let's get into what this actually is, how the math works, and where the whole system is starting to crack.

## The Basic Mechanic (It's Simpler Than It Sounds)

Your solar panels don't produce electricity at a perfectly steady rate that matches your household's demand minute by minute. On a sunny Tuesday in May, while you're at work, your panels might be cranking out 4 kilowatts and your house is only using 800 watts to keep the fridge running and the router on. That surplus has to go somewhere.

Without net metering, it just... goes. Your inverter would curtail the production, or you'd need a battery to absorb it. But with net metering, that excess electricity flows back into the utility grid, and your meter runs backward. Your utility essentially credits you for what you sent them.

Then at night, when your panels are producing nothing and you're running the dishwasher and watching TV, you pull power from the grid. The utility draws down your credit balance to cover it.

At the end of the billing period, the utility does the math: generation sent to the grid minus consumption pulled from it. If you've sent more than you've taken, you've got a "net export." If you've taken more than you've sent, you owe the difference. That's the whole concept.

What surprised me when I first dug into the actual billing mechanics is how much variation exists in that final step. The word "credit" sounds simple, but the rate at which utilities compensate your excess generation is where policies diverge wildly.

Under full retail-rate net metering, which is what many states mandated for years, the credit you earn for exporting a kilowatt-hour equals exactly what you'd pay to import one. If your utility charges $0.14/kWh, you get a $0.14 credit for every kWh you export. The math is clean and favorable.

Under that structure, it genuinely doesn't matter much when you use electricity. You can export during the day and import at night at a 1:1 exchange rate. The grid essentially acts as a free battery.

But a growing number of states and utilities have moved to "avoided cost" compensation, where exports are credited at the utility's wholesale rate, which is often $0.03 to $0.06 per kilowatt-hour. In those markets, exporting power earns you a fraction of what it costs to buy it back. The economics of a basic rooftop system without battery storage get considerably worse.

California's NEM 3.0, which went into effect in April 2023, is the most visible example of this shift. The new structure cut average export rates by roughly 75% compared to NEM 2.0, according to reporting from multiple California solar advocacy groups. The CPUC's own analysis projected that solar payback periods in the state would extend from an average of around 6 years to somewhere between 9 and 12 years for typical systems. That's a real hit. It also sent a pretty clear signal to the rest of the country that full retail-rate net metering isn't a guaranteed future.

This is one of those things installers sometimes gloss over when they're showing you the 25-year savings projection. Always ask: what credit rate is baked into that calculation?

## What Actually Happens Month to Month

Here's a walkthrough of how net metering billing plays out in practice, because the theory is one thing and the actual statement is another.

Say your utility is in a full retail-rate net metering state and charges $0.13/kWh. In July, your 8 kW system produces 1,100 kWh. Your household uses 900 kWh. You export 200 kWh to the grid, earning $26 in credit.

In December, your system produces 380 kWh. You use 950 kWh. You pull 570 kWh from the grid. At $0.13/kWh, that's $74.10 owed, minus whatever credit balance you've carried over from sunnier months.

Most utilities operate on annual true-up billing. Credits accumulate across the year, and once a year, the utility settles the account. If you've got a net surplus at year's end, some utilities pay it out (often at a lower "excess generation" rate), and others just zero out the balance. That part matters: if your system is significantly oversized and you're banking big annual surpluses, you may be generating electricity that essentially earns you nothing. Sizing your system to cover around 100% of annual consumption, not 120% or 140%, is usually the smarter financial move.

There are also fixed monthly charges to watch for. Utilities in many states have started adding "grid access fees" for solar customers, sometimes called standby charges, that can run $15 to $50 per month regardless of your net usage. These don't show up in the per-kWh credit math, but they quietly erode your savings.

## Where Net Metering Stands Right Now, State by State

Forty-one states plus Washington D.C. have some form of mandatory net metering policy, according to [EnergySage's market data](https://news.energysage.com/). But "some form" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. The policies range from genuinely excellent (Massachusetts, New Jersey, Minnesota) to technically-net-metering-but-not-really (some southeastern states where utilities have found creative ways to limit or delay credits).

A few things I'd look for when evaluating your state's policy:

The compensation rate (retail vs. avoided cost vs. something in between) is the big one. Net metering carryover rules: can you bank credits monthly, or do they expire? Whether there's a capacity cap on the state program, because some states freeze enrollment once solar reaches a certain percentage of peak demand. And interconnection timelines, which sounds bureaucratic but in states like Florida or parts of Texas, slow interconnection approvals can delay your activation by 3 to 6 months.

The [U.S. Department of Energy's homeowner solar guide](https://www.energy.gov/eere/solar/homeowners-guide-going-solar) has a good breakdown of what to look for in your specific utility's tariff, and it's worth spending 20 minutes there before signing anything.

Hawaii, notably, eliminated traditional net metering entirely in 2015. Customers now choose between self-supply (no export allowed at all) and a grid-supply option with low export rates. The result: battery storage adoption there is extraordinarily high, because you basically have to have one to make solar work financially without net metering.

That trajectory is worth paying attention to. Hawaii went first. California's NEM 3.0 was next. The question is which states follow, and when.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does net metering mean my electric bill will be zero?

Possibly, but not guaranteed. If your system is sized correctly and your utility has full retail-rate net metering, many homeowners do reach near-zero bills. But most utilities still charge a monthly minimum or a fixed grid-access fee, typically $10 to $30, even for customers with a net surplus.

### What happens to my net metering credits if I move?

This depends on your state and utility, but in most cases, net metering credits are attached to the account and meter address, not to you personally. Some utilities allow you to transfer credits to the new owner as part of a home sale, but many just zero the balance out at the end of the annual true-up period. If you're moving within a year of installation, this is worth asking your utility about directly.

### Can I get net metering with a battery storage system?

Yes, and in NEM 3.0 states like California, you pretty much need to pair storage with solar to make the economics work. The battery lets you store excess daytime production and use it at night rather than exporting it at the low avoided-cost rate and buying it back later at the higher retail rate. In strong retail net metering states, the financial case for adding a battery is weaker since the grid already acts as your free virtual battery.

### Is net metering the same everywhere in the U.S.?

No, and this is where a lot of homeowners get tripped up by national averages. Net metering rules are set at the state level and sometimes at the individual utility level. Two neighbors in different utility service territories can have dramatically different credit rates, carryover rules, and program caps, even within the same state.

### What's the difference between net metering and a feed-in tariff?

A feed-in tariff (FIT) pays you a fixed rate for every kilowatt-hour you generate and export, separate from what you pay for electricity you consume. Net metering offsets your consumption bill with credits for exports. Feed-in tariffs were more common in Europe and in early U.S. programs; most current U.S. residential solar incentives run through net metering rather than FITs, though the lines blur a bit in avoided-cost compensation models.

---

The honest takeaway from all of this is that net metering is genuinely good in many states and genuinely mediocre in others, and the gap between those situations is large enough to change your system sizing strategy, your battery decision, and your realistic payback timeline. Before you get anywhere near signing a solar contract, pull up your utility's interconnection tariff and find the words "net energy metering" or "distributed generation compensation." The rate per kWh and the carryover rules are in there. They matter more than the brand of panels on your roof.
