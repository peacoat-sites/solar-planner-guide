## Two Warranties, Not One

This is where most people get confused, and I get it. The language sounds interchangeable. It isn't.

Your [solar panels](/solar-panels-in-illinois-guide/) come with a **product warranty** (sometimes called a materials or equipment warranty) and a **performance warranty** (sometimes called a power output or linear degradation warranty). They cover completely different things.

The product warranty covers physical defects: delamination, cracking, junction box failures, frame corrosion. Most reputable manufacturers offer 12 to 15 years here. Some, like Panasonic and SunPower, offer 25 years on materials. That gap, 12 years versus 25, is meaningful when you're putting something on your roof that you expect to last three decades.

The performance warranty is the one people actually quote when they say "25-year warranty." It guarantees your panels will produce at least a certain percentage of their original rated output after a given number of years. The standard used to be 80% at 25 years, which sounds fine until you do the math. If your 400-watt panel is only guaranteed to produce 320 watts after 25 years, that's a 2% degradation per year if most of the drop happens early. The better manufacturers have moved to linear degradation models, where the annual loss is capped uniformly, typically at 0.5% per year after a first-year drop of around 2%.

The [National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL)](https://www.nrel.gov/) has published research showing that real-world panel degradation averages about 0.5% per year for modern panels, which means the better warranties are basically promising what panels already do naturally. The weaker ones are promising less. That tells you something.

## The Installer Warranty Nobody Reads

Here's what I tell people when they're comparing bids: the installer's workmanship warranty is just as important as the manufacturer's. Maybe more, because roof penetrations, wiring connections, and mounting hardware failures are far more common in the first ten years than actual panel defects.

Workmanship warranties cover installation errors: a leaky roof penetration, a loose conduit, a combiner box that wasn't waterproofed properly. Industry standard is somewhere between 5 and 10 years. The better installers offer 10 years. Some regional companies offer 25-year workmanship warranties, though I'd honestly want to think hard about whether a company founded in 2022 is going to be around in 2047.

This brings up the uncomfortable part of the conversation. Installer bankruptcy is real. SEIA data shows that installer turnover has been a persistent problem in residential solar. If your installer goes under, your workmanship warranty disappears with them. Full stop. The manufacturer warranty survives (assuming the manufacturer itself stays solvent), but someone still has to physically come out and fix the roof leak or the failed combiner box. That's now your problem and your expense.

Ask every installer two questions before signing: "What happens to my workmanship warranty if your company closes?" and "Are you backed by a bond or a third-party warranty administrator?" Some larger national installers, like Sunrun and Sunpower, have warranty protection programs specifically because they've been around long enough to know this is a real issue.

## Reading the Actual Document (Yes, You Should)

I know. Nobody wants to read a PDF that looks like a mortgage. But there are four specific things worth scanning for, and it takes about ten minutes.

**Transferability.** If you sell your house, does the warranty transfer to the new owner? Most manufacturers allow it once, with a notification period (often 30 to 90 days after sale). Qcells explicitly allows transfer. Some smaller manufacturers charge a fee. This matters for your home's resale value, and real estate agents in solar-heavy markets are starting to ask about it.

**Claim process.** How do you actually make a claim? Some manufacturers require the original installer to submit claims on your behalf. If that installer is gone, you could be stuck. The better manufacturers (REC, Panasonic, Qcells) allow homeowners to submit directly.

**Exclusions.** Standard exclusions include damage from hail above a certain size (usually 1 inch diameter), flooding, fire, and "improper installation," which is a phrase that's done a lot of work in disputed claims. If the manufacturer can argue your installer put things in wrong, your product warranty claim can get denied even if the defect is obvious.

**Dispute resolution clause.** Some manufacturer warranties include binding arbitration clauses that prevent you from suing in court. I don't have a strong opinion on whether that's a dealbreaker, but you should know it's there.

Scenario: A homeowner in Atlanta noticed micro-cracking on two panels after five years. She submitted a claim directly to the manufacturer. The manufacturer initially responded that the cracking was due to "improper installation" and denied the claim. She escalated, citing NREL research on manufacturing-defect cracking patterns, and the manufacturer reversed and replaced the panels. The lesson: know your exclusions, and be prepared to push back with documentation.

## Manufacturer Longevity Is Not a Warranty Feature, But It Should Be

Nobody puts "we might go bankrupt" in their warranty document, but it's the single biggest risk to your 25-year coverage.

Chinese manufacturers now produce a large share of the world's solar panels, and many of them offer aggressive warranties with terms that look better than the competition's. Some are legitimate, well-capitalized companies. Others are not. The ones that aren't tend to disappear during market contractions or antidumping tariff cycles, and there have been several of those. As of August 2026, the tariff environment on imported panels is still complicated, and some smaller manufacturers are under genuine financial pressure.

This is why I lean toward recommending panels from manufacturers with a long U.S. market track record or with U.S.-based manufacturing operations. Qcells, for instance, has a manufacturing facility in Dalton, Georgia. That's not a guarantee, but it's a signal of commitment to the U.S. market that matters when you're thinking about whether someone will honor a claim in 2041.

Scenario: A homeowner in New Jersey had panels from a Chinese manufacturer that exited the U.S. market in 2023. His product warranty was technically still "valid" under the original document, but with no U.S. subsidiary to contact and no authorized service agents, he had no practical recourse when three panels failed. His installer had to source replacement panels of similar specs out of pocket, and only covered part of the cost. His new total system output after replacement was still about 4% lower than original because the replacement panels weren't a perfect match.
