The federal solar tax credit is worth $0 if you don't understand how it actually works. That's not a scare tactic — it's what happens when homeowners assume "tax credit" means a check in the mail, or that they'll automatically capture the full 30% in year one no matter what their tax bill looks like.

Let's fix that.

## What the 30% Credit Actually Is (and Isn't)

The Residential Clean Energy Credit — the official name almost nobody uses — lets you claim 30% of your total solar installation cost against your federal income tax liability. You installed a $25,000 system? You can claim $7,500 off what you owe the IRS. Not a deduction. Not a rebate. A dollar-for-dollar reduction of your tax bill.

That distinction matters enormously. A $7,500 deduction on a 22% marginal rate saves you $1,650. A $7,500 credit saves you $7,500. They are not the same thing, and a surprising number of homeowners mix them up.

What the credit is *not*: it's not a check. If your credit exceeds your tax liability for the year, the IRS won't send you the difference. But here's the part that saves people real money if they know it — the excess rolls forward. You can carry the unused portion to future tax years until the credit phases out.

Right now, the 30% rate is locked in through 2032, thanks to the Inflation Reduction Act passed in August 2022. After that, it drops to 26% in 2033, 22% in 2034, and goes away entirely for residential installations in 2035 — unless Congress acts again. For 2026, you're squarely in the 30% window. No guessing, no cliff edge this year.

The system has to be new and installed at a U.S. residence you own. Not a rental property you lease out. Not your landlord's building. Your home, which can be a house, condo, cooperative, mobile home, or houseboat. The [Solar Energy Industries Association (SEIA)](https://www.seia.org/) notes that the home doesn't even have to be your primary residence — a vacation home counts, with some caveats around tax liability allocation.

What's included in that 30%:

- Solar panels themselves
- Labor costs for installation (this is one people miss — labor counts)
- Wiring, inverters, mounting hardware
- Battery storage, if it's charged exclusively by solar (this changed with the IRA — standalone storage that meets certain size requirements now qualifies even without solar, but paired storage is straightforward)
- Permitting and inspection fees
- Sales tax on all eligible equipment

That last one is underrated. On a $25,000 system in a state with a 6% sales tax, you're looking at $1,500 in tax — and 30% of that $1,500 comes back to you too. Small, but real.

What doesn't count: your roof replacement. Even if the installer tells you the roof needs to go before they'll put panels up, the roofing cost itself isn't eligible. Only the portion directly related to solar installation is — so if you have to beef up specific sections of roof decking to support panel mounts, that narrow portion might qualify, but a full tear-off and re-shingle does not.

## The Tax Liability Problem Nobody Warns You About

Here's where things go sideways for a specific group of homeowners: retirees, part-time workers, people with heavy deductions, or anyone whose federal tax liability is genuinely low.

Say you're retired with a comfortable income but significant deductions. Your federal tax liability ends up at $3,000 a year. You install a $30,000 solar system and expect a $9,000 credit. You'll get it — just not all at once. You'd claim $3,000 this year, carry $6,000 forward, claim $3,000 next year, $3,000 the year after. Three years to fully capture the credit.

That's fine, as long as you plan for it. The problem is homeowners who factor the full credit into year-one payback calculations without checking whether their tax bill actually supports it. I've seen people buy systems expecting the credit to effectively cut their loan balance in half immediately — then discover they owe $1,800 to the IRS, not $9,000, and their cash flow math collapses.

Before you sign a contract, pull your last two years of tax returns. Look at line 24 on your Form 1040 — that's your total tax. That number, more or less, is your annual capacity to absorb the credit. If it's lower than your expected credit, run the carryforward math and make sure the payback timeline still works for you.

One thing worth knowing: the credit doesn't interact with the Alternative Minimum Tax the way it used to. Under older rules, AMT could eat into your credit. Post-IRA, the Residential Clean Energy Credit can offset both regular tax and AMT liability. That's a genuine improvement, particularly for higher earners.

## How to Actually Claim It

You claim the credit on IRS Form 5695 — "Residential Energy Credits." It's not complicated, but it requires you to keep good records.

The process, stripped down:

Get your final signed contract and invoice showing total project cost. Keep the permit and inspection documentation. If you're claiming battery storage, have documentation that it's solar-charged (your installer can usually provide this). When you file your taxes, complete Form 5695 Part I, calculate 30% of your qualified costs, and carry that number to Schedule 3 of your 1040, which flows into your total tax computation.

If you're using tax software like TurboTax or H&R Block, there's a specific section for energy credits — it'll walk you through Form 5695 with prompts. If your situation is at all complicated (significant carryforward, combined solar-plus-storage, system on a second home), a CPA who handles residential energy credits is worth the $200-300. The credit itself is straightforward; the edge cases less so.

One thing that trips people up: the credit is based on when installation is *completed and placed in service*, not when you signed the contract or paid the deposit. If you sign in November 2025 but installation finishes in January 2026, that's a 2026 tax year credit, claimed when you file in early 2027. Don't assume the year you paid is the year you claim.

The [U.S. Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/eere/solar/homeowners-guide-going-solar) has a clean summary of the placed-in-service rule if you want the official language.

## 2026 Specifically: What's Changed, What Hasn't

The rate is 30%. That hasn't changed and won't change this year. But a few things are worth noting for people making decisions right now.

Battery storage has been a moving target. The IRA clarified that standalone battery systems (not paired with solar) qualify for the 30% credit if they have a capacity of at least 3 kilowatt-hours. For 2026 installations, this is settled law — a Tesla Powerwall 3, an Enphase IQ Battery 5P, or a Franklin WH5000 can each qualify even if you install them on an existing solar system rather than a new one. That's a big deal for homeowners who went solar in 2020-2023 and have been on the fence about adding storage.

There's been some political noise about whether the IRA provisions could be clawed back. As of mid-2025, the 30% residential credit has remained intact through various budget discussions, though the commercial and utility-scale provisions have faced more scrutiny. Residential clean energy credits have historically been bipartisan in a way commercial incentives haven't. I wouldn't bet against the credit surviving through 2032 as scheduled, but I also wouldn't wait until 2031 to go solar based on that assumption alone.

## State Credits Stack On Top

Federal gets all the attention, but your state's incentives stack on top of it. California's NEM 3.0 changed the net metering math significantly, but the state still has property tax exclusions and utility rebate programs. New York has a 25% state tax credit (capped at $5,000). Massachusetts offers a 15% credit on top of federal. Texas has no state income tax, so no state credit to stack, but strong property tax exemptions on added home value.

The key mechanic: most state credits calculate off the *pre-federal-credit* system cost, not the post-credit cost. So your state credit and federal credit both apply to the same gross number. That's genuinely additive, not diminishing.

SEIA maintains a database of state incentives that's reasonably current — worth cross-referencing with the DSIRE database run by NC State before you finalize any numbers.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I claim the federal solar tax credit if I lease my solar panels?

No. The 30% credit goes to whoever owns the system. If you lease panels or sign a power purchase agreement (PPA), the installer or third-party owner claims the credit, not you. This is one of the strongest financial arguments for buying outright or financing rather than leasing.

### What if my tax credit is larger than my tax liability this year?

The unused portion carries forward to the following tax year. You can keep carrying it forward until the credit phases out in 2035. There's no refund option — it's nonrefundable — but the carryforward provision means you won't lose the money, just delay it.

### Does adding a battery storage system to an existing solar installation qualify for the 30% credit?

Yes, as of the Inflation Reduction Act. A battery with at least 3 kWh of capacity qualifies for the 30% credit in 2026, whether it's paired with new solar or added to an existing system. The battery must be charged by a renewable source (solar qualifies). Keep documentation from your installer confirming this.

### Does the solar tax credit affect my home's cost basis or capital gains when I sell?

Technically, yes. The IRS requires you to reduce your home's cost basis by the amount of the federal tax credit you claimed. In practice, this matters mainly if you sell the home for a large enough gain to exceed the $250,000/$500,000 capital gains exclusion for primary residences, which most homeowners won't hit. Worth mentioning to your accountant if you're close.

### Can I claim the credit for solar installed on a vacation home or second property?

Yes, provided you own the property and it's a qualifying residence (not a pure rental). The credit can be split between a primary and secondary home if you're allocating costs, but you need to make sure your total tax liability across both properties supports the claim. A tax professional is worth consulting if you're splitting across residences.
