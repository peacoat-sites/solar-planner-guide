At RE+ 2026 on November 17, EG4 announced something the residential solar market hasn't seen before: a complete 14.4 kW solar-plus-storage system priced at $1.20 per watt for the solar component. The full Homestead 60 package, which includes 60 kWh of [battery storage](/best-home-battery/) and four ground-mount racks, starts at $20,499. That works out to $342 per usable kilowatt-hour of storage. For context, the national installed average for residential solar in 2026 sits between $2.50 and $3.50 per watt. EG4 didn't just nudge that number. They cut it roughly in half.

The timing is pointed. The 30% federal 25D residential clean [energy credit](/srec-solar-renewable-energy-credit/), extended through 2032 under the Inflation Reduction Act, is currently scheduled to step down to 26% in 2033 and 22% in 2034. Homeowners who go solar while the 30% credit remains in effect can knock nearly a third off their total cost at tax time.  So when a system shows up claiming $1.20/watt for solar, it's worth taking seriously, even if you have to ask some hard questions about what "complete" actually means.

## What You Get for $20,499

The Homestead 60 is prewired and preassembled, which is not a small thing. Most DIY solar kits hand you a pile of components and a manual. EG4 ships four ground-mount racks with the solar array and battery storage already integrated at the factory level, according to the product listing on Signature Solar's site. That cuts installation labor significantly, at least in theory.

Here's how the numbers stack against what a contractor quote typically looks like in 2026, now that the 25D credit is off the table:

The gap is not marginal. Even if you add $8,000–$12,000 for professional installation of the EG4 system, you're still likely well under a fully contracted quote for comparable capacity. The 60 kWh storage figure especially stands out. Most installer-bundled systems pair solar with 10–20 kWh of storage. Getting to 60 kWh through a contractor would cost considerably more.

## The FEOC Question and Why It Matters Now

EG4 is positioning the Homestead 60 as FEOC-compliant, meaning its components don't originate from entities flagged under the Inflation Reduction Act's Foreign Entity of Concern rules. That matters because any future federal [incentive programs](/utility-solar-incentive-programs/) will almost certainly carry the same sourcing requirements the IRA established. Buying non-compliant equipment now could leave you locked out of rebates or credits that Congress might restore.

This isn't a hypothetical concern. FEOC compliance has already tripped up commercial solar projects trying to claim the investment tax credit. For a homeowner putting $20,000-plus into a system they expect to run for 25 years, knowing your hardware checks the sourcing box is a real financial consideration, not just regulatory trivia.

## The Honest Downsides

Ground-mount systems aren't for everyone. You need land, and you need to run conduit from the array to your home's electrical panel. Permitting for ground-mount installs can be more involved than rooftop in some jurisdictions. Homeowners with small lots, restrictive HOAs, or complex terrain may find the Homestead 60's format simply doesn't fit their property.

There's also the installation reality. "Prewired and preassembled" reduces your labor, but it doesn't eliminate the need for a licensed electrician to connect the system to your panel and handle the utility interconnection. That cost varies widely by region. In some markets you're looking at $3,000–$6,000 just for that final connection work.

And the system isn't shipping yet. EG4 debuted the Homestead 60 at RE+ 2026, but shipments don't start until Q1 2027. You're putting down a $5,000 deposit to reserve a unit that won't arrive for at least two to four months. That's normal for new hardware launches, but it's worth factoring into your timeline, especially if you're trying to maximize any state-level incentives with their own deadlines.

## Who This Actually Makes Sense For

Rural homeowners are the clearest fit. If you've got open land, an agricultural property, or a home where rooftop solar isn't practical, a ground-mount kit at $1.20/watt is a genuinely different conversation than it was 18 months ago. The 60 kWh storage capacity also makes this relevant for people thinking about full or partial off-grid setups, backup during extended outages, or pairing with an EV charging load that would otherwise hammer the grid.

State-level incentives also matter significantly for people in states with strong net metering policies or state-level solar tax credits. State incentives carry a meaningful share of the ROI calculation alongside the federal credit. A homeowner in a state like New York or Massachusetts, where state incentives can still offset 25% or more of costs, starts the pencil-out from a very different position than someone in a state with minimal solar policy.

Homeowners evaluating solar should pay close attention to payback periods, since the upfront equipment and installation cost drives how long it takes to break even, particularly if federal or state incentives are reduced or unavailable for a given purchase. That makes the Homestead 60's pricing directly relevant to whether solar still pencils out in 2026.

The EG4 Homestead 60 doesn't fix every problem in residential solar economics, and it won't suit every property. But $1.20/watt for a code-compliant, FEOC-ready, ground-mount system is a real data point that changes the comparison shopping process. If you're getting contractor quotes right now, run this number against them before you sign anything.
