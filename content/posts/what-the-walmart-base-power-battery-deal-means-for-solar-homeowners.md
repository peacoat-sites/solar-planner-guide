Walmart started selling home [battery subscriptions](/battery-as-a-service-what-the-new-0-down-storage-leases-mean-for-solar/) this month. That's not a headline you'd have expected two years ago, and it signals something real is shifting in how Americans access energy storage. On October 30, 2026, Walmart announced a strategic investment in Base Power, a Texas-based company that installs, owns, and operates home batteries, then signs up customers as retail electricity subscribers. In-store sign-ups began at Walmart and Sam's Club locations in November 2026, with enrollees getting 20% off Base Power's standard installation fee.

For solar homeowners, the timing is pointed. The federal 30% residential [solar tax credit](/itc-solar-investment-tax-credit-explained/) under Section 25D was eliminated when the One Big Beautiful Bill was signed in July 2026, with the credit ending December 31, 2025 as the effective expiration date written into that legislation. Net metering rates are shrinking in state after state. Arizona's export rate for new APS and UNS interconnections dropped roughly 10% to about $0.0555/kWh as of September 1, 2026. If you're generating solar power and exporting it to the grid, you're getting paid less for it. That changes the math on batteries, and Base Power's model is designed exactly for that moment.

Base Power already has more than 35,000 members in Texas and over 20,000 home batteries deployed. The company monetizes that network by discharging stored energy into the grid during price spikes, which is how it can offer customers a relatively low monthly lease, reportedly in the range of $19 to $29, on top of a reduced upfront installation fee. It acts as the household's retail electricity provider. That's a different business model than buying a Powerwall.

## Why the "Company-Owned" Battery Model Matters Now

The traditional path to home storage looks like this: buy panels, buy a battery, claim the tax credit, own everything. That worked well when the 30% [federal credit](/the-prepaid-solar-lease-is-now-the-smartest-way-to-go-solar/) was available and when net metering paid close to retail rates for excess generation. Both of those conditions have weakened significantly.

Base Power's model flips the ownership question. You don't own the battery. You pay a monthly fee and get backup power plus, in most cases, reduced electricity bills. Base retains control over when the battery discharges to the grid. For homeowners who don't want to manage a large capital purchase right now, that's genuinely appealing. For solar homeowners specifically, it raises a question worth sitting with: who benefits more from arbitrage, you or the company that owns the hardware?

The honest answer depends on your electricity rates, your solar production, and how aggressive your state's time-of-use pricing is. In Texas, where Base Power operates today, real-time pricing swings can be dramatic. Discharging a battery at peak prices can generate real value. Outside Texas, in regulated states with flatter rate structures, the arbitrage value is smaller and the business model works differently.

## The Shrinking Export Rate Problem, Illustrated

Net metering cuts are happening faster than most solar owners realize. Arizona's September 2026 reduction is one recent example, but it's part of a national pattern that's been building since California's NEM 3.0 took effect in 2023. When export rates drop, the financial case for oversizing a solar array weakens. It also makes self-consumption, using what you generate rather than exporting it, more valuable.

That's where storage becomes a real tool, not just a backup option. Here's how the economics shift depending on your approach:

| Scenario | Export Rate | Battery Ownership | Monthly Cost | Self-Consumption Benefit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Solar only, high NEM rate | ~$0.10–$0.12/kWh | None | $0 storage cost | Low priority |
| Solar only, APS/UNS post-Sept 2026 | ~$0.0555/kWh | None | $0 storage cost | High priority |
| Solar + owned battery (no tax credit) | ~$0.0555/kWh | Homeowner | Higher upfront | High |
| Solar + Base Power subscription | ~$0.0555/kWh | Base Power | ~$19–$29/month | High, shared arbitrage |

The table shows why the Base Power model is landing at this particular moment. When export rates were generous, you could overbuild your solar array and let the grid act as your free battery. That calculation is less favorable now. A subscription battery at $19 to $29 a month may outperform doing nothing, even if it doesn't match the long-term returns of owned storage.

## What It Costs to Own Versus Subscribe

A fully owned home battery system, say a 13.5 kWh Tesla Powerwall 3, runs roughly $11,500 to $15,000 installed before incentives, based on current market pricing. Without the 30% federal tax credit that expired in 2025, there's no longer a $3,450 to $4,500 federal offset for most buyers. Some state-level incentives exist, but they're inconsistent.

At $29/month, Base Power's subscription costs about $348/year. Over 10 years, that's $3,480, not counting the upfront installation fee (which Walmart's deal discounts by 20%). An owned system depreciates over time but retains asset value and gives you full control. A subscribed system has no resale value and ties your energy supply to a third-party company's business continuity.

Multiple bills have been filed in Congress since July 2026 to restore some version of the solar tax credits, according to reporting from Free Press's November 8 solar news roundup. If any of those pass, the math on ownership shifts again. That policy uncertainty is real, and it cuts both ways: it's a reason some homeowners are waiting before committing to an owned system, and it's a reason others are locking into subscriptions now rather than betting on a credit that may or may not return.

## Who Should Actually Consider Base Power

Base Power is currently a Texas product. If you're not in Texas, you can't enroll today, and expansion timelines haven't been announced publicly. That's the first filter.

Within Texas, the model suits a specific profile: homeowners who want backup power and bill predictability, don't want to manage a large capital purchase, and are comfortable with their battery being dispatched for grid services they don't directly control. If you have an existing solar system and your export rates have dropped, a subscription battery can still boost self-consumption even if you're not getting full arbitrage value.

It's less appealing for homeowners who want to maximize the financial return on storage, plan to sell their home in the next few years (a leased battery complicates the sale), or live in a state with strong remaining solar incentives that reward ownership. A November 2026 analysis cited by Free Press found that rooftop solar paired with batteries could save the U.S. power system $614 billion through 2050 and cut the average household bill by about $122 per year. That's a system-level finding, not a guarantee for any individual installation, but it suggests the direction the market is heading.

The Walmart channel is worth watching regardless of whether you'd personally subscribe. When a retailer with 4,600 U.S. stores starts signing up battery customers at the customer service desk, storage becomes a mass-market product. That tends to push prices down over time, including for people who eventually want to own.
