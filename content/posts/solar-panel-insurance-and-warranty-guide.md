[Most homeowners](/the-july-4-solar-deadline-most-homeowners-have-never-heard-of/) spend weeks comparing panel brands and installer quotes, then sign whatever warranty documents land in front of them without reading a word. That's a mistake that can cost thousands when something goes wrong ten years in. Solar warranties and insurance coverage are genuinely complicated, and the gap between what you think you're protected against and what you're actually covered for is often significant.

The short version: you'll typically have three separate warranties on a residential solar system (equipment, performance, and workmanship), plus two distinct insurance questions (does your homeowner's policy cover the panels, and do you need separate solar-specific coverage?). None of these automatically cover everything, and they don't all come from the same place.

## The Three Warranties You'll Actually Receive

**Equipment (product) warranty.** This covers manufacturing defects, premature failure, and hardware malfunctions. Standard terms from established manufacturers run 10 to 25 years. SunPower, for instance, offers a 25-year combined product and performance warranty. Qcells and REC Group commonly offer 12 to 25-year product warranties depending on the product line. What this doesn't cover: damage from hail, fallen branches, or a roofer's boot. That's a property damage question, which falls to your insurance.

**Performance (power output) warranty.** Separate from the equipment warranty, this guarantees that your panels will produce at least a specified percentage of their rated wattage over time. The industry floor is roughly 80% of rated output at year 25, with annual degradation capped at around 0.5% per year. Some premium manufacturers have tightened this to 0.25% annual degradation and 92% output at year 25, which is meaningfully better. The [Solar Energy Industries Association (SEIA)](https://www.seia.org/) notes that typical real-world degradation rates align with these warranty figures, running 0.5% to 0.8% per year on average.

**Workmanship warranty.** This one comes from your installer, not the panel manufacturer, and it's the most variable piece. It covers installation errors: roof penetrations that leak, wiring that wasn't done correctly, racking that shifts. Ranges run from 1 year (too short, avoid this) to 10 or even 25 years from larger national installers. Sunrun and Sunpower's direct installation arm offer 10-year workmanship coverage. Many local installers offer 5 years. Some offer 2. The length matters less than the backing: if the company folds, so does the warranty unless it's backed by an insurance bond or a third-party warranty administrator.

Always ask: "If your company closes tomorrow, what happens to my workmanship warranty?" If the answer isn't specific, that's a red flag.

## Inverter Warranties Deserve Their Own Paragraph

Inverters fail more often than panels. [String inverters](/microinverters-vs-string-inverters/) from SolarEdge typically carry a 12-year standard warranty, extendable to 20 or 25 years for a fee. Microinverters (Enphase IQ series) often come with 25-year warranties because each unit handles lower load. If your quote includes a string inverter with only a 10-year warranty on a 25-year system, budget for a likely replacement around year 12 to 15. That's a real cost, often $1,000 to $3,000 installed, that payback calculators frequently omit.

## What Your Homeowner's Insurance Does (and Doesn't) Cover

| Scenario | Typically Covered? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hail damage to panels | Yes, under dwelling coverage | May require raised dwelling limit |
| Fire damage (including from inverter malfunction) | Yes | Confirm system is listed with insurer |
| Theft of panels | Yes, under dwelling or personal property | Less common but it happens |
| Hurricane/wind damage | Usually yes, check your policy exclusions | Flood damage is separate |
| Output loss from shade (neighbor's new tree) | No | Not an insurable event |
| Manufacturing defect causing no power | No | Covered by equipment warranty |
| Installer error causing roof leak | Possibly | May fall to workmanship warranty or liability |
| Panels damaged during roof replacement | Possibly | Roofer's liability insurance, not yours |

Panels attached to your home are almost always classified as part of the dwelling under a standard homeowner's policy, which means they're covered up to your dwelling limit. The catch is that most people set their dwelling limit years before adding solar. A 10kW system adds $25,000 to $40,000 of replacement value to your home structure. If you're already close to your policy ceiling, you need to call your insurer and raise the limit before the system goes live. The [U.S. Department of Energy](https://www.energy.gov/eere/solar/homeowners-guide-going-solar) recommends notifying your homeowner's insurance company before or immediately after installation for exactly this reason.

A smaller but real concern: some insurers have started surcharging or even declining to renew policies on homes with solar, particularly in high-fire-risk states like California. Check with your insurer early in the process. Don't assume the conversation can wait until after installation.

## Leased Systems and TPPAs: The Insurance Wrinkle Nobody Mentions

If you're leasing panels or on a power purchase agreement (PPA), the solar company typically owns the hardware and carries its own insurance on the equipment. That sounds convenient. The problem is you're still living under panels that could be damaged in a storm, and the interaction between your homeowner's policy and the leasing company's coverage can get messy. Some homeowner's insurers will not cover third-party-owned equipment under your dwelling policy at all. Get the lease agreement's insurance provisions in writing, and share them with your insurance agent before signing. The two policies need to be coordinated, not assumed to automatically work together.

## Reading a Warranty Before You Sign: What to Actually Check

Most people don't read the warranty document until something breaks. By then it's too late to negotiate. When you're comparing installers, ask for the full warranty documentation as PDFs, not just the summary sheet. Run through these specific items:

1. **Who is the warrantor?** The manufacturer for equipment/performance, the installer for workmanship. If a third-party administrator is backing the workmanship warranty, get their name and look them up.
2. **What's the claims process?** Some manufacturers require you to submit a detailed report with performance data logs, panel serial numbers, and photos. If you don't have a monitoring system installed, you may not be able to satisfy the burden of proof. A home energy monitor like the [Emporia Vue Energy Monitor](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=emporia+vue+energy+monitor) (affiliate link, the site may earn a commission) can generate the production logs you'd need for a warranty claim.
3. **Is there a labor coverage component?** Panel manufacturers often cover the replacement panel, but not the labor to remove, ship, and reinstall it. Some don't cover shipping either. On a rooftop install, labor alone for a panel swap can run $200 to $600.
4. **What voids the warranty?** Common disqualifiers include unauthorized modifications, damage from "acts of God" (which is why insurance exists), and failure to maintain the system per the manual.
5. **Is the performance warranty prorated?** Some are. If the warranty pays out at a declining rate as panels age, the compensation for a failure in year 22 may be nearly nothing.

**Example:** Assume you have panels rated at 400W each, with a performance warranty guaranteeing 80% output at year 25. That means 320W minimum. If testing shows a panel producing 290W at year 20, it's failed the performance threshold (the cumulative degradation curve should still be above 310W at that point using 0.5%/year). To file a claim, you'd need your monitoring data showing the production shortfall, the panel serial number, and your original purchase documentation. Some manufacturers process this entirely online; others require a certified technician's report.
