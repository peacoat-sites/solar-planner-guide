[Most homeowners](/the-july-4-solar-deadline-most-homeowners-have-never-heard-of/) shopping for solar panels fixate on price per panel and forget that wattage is the number that actually determines how much electricity their system produces. A 400-watt panel and a 320-watt panel from the same manufacturer are not interchangeable just because they look identical on a roof. The difference compounds across a 25-year system life, and picking the wrong configuration can mean undersizing your system by 20% or more.

Wattage, technically called "rated power" or "peak power output," tells you how many watts a panel generates under Standard Test Conditions (STC): 1,000 watts of irradiance per square meter, 25°C cell temperature, and 1.5 air mass spectrum. Real-world output is always lower. How much lower depends on your climate, roof angle, shading, and the ambient temperature where you live. This gap is not a flaw or a dealer trick. It's physics.

## What "rated wattage" actually means

STC is a lab standard, not a rooftop. When a panel is rated at 400W, that means it hits 400W under those specific controlled conditions. On a hot summer afternoon, when your roof surface might be 50°C or higher, cell temperature rises and output drops. Most silicon panels lose roughly 0.3% to 0.5% of their rated output for every degree Celsius above 25°C (this spec is called the temperature coefficient, and it's on every panel datasheet). A panel with a temperature coefficient of -0.35%/°C on a 60°C cell surface loses about 12% of its rated power right there.

The figure that corrects for real-world conditions is called PVOUT or the "production estimate," and the National [Renewable Energy](/srec-solar-renewable-energy-credit/) Laboratory (NREL) has mapped solar resource data for every county in the country through its PVWatts Calculator. That tool applies a default "derate factor" of around 0.86, meaning it expects you'll lose roughly 14% of nameplate capacity to things like wiring losses, inverter inefficiency, soiling, and temperature. Using PVWatts before you sign anything is free and takes about five minutes.

## Panel wattage ranges: what's on the market today

Residential [solar panels](/how-many-solar-panels-to-power-a-house/) have gotten noticeably more powerful over the past decade. Standard 60-cell panels used to cap out around 270–310W. Today's mainstream 60-cell and 120-half-cell designs routinely hit 380–430W, and some manufacturers are shipping 500W+ panels for residential use. Here's a practical breakdown of what you'll encounter:

| Panel Class | Typical Wattage | Common Use Case | Approx. Cost per Watt (panel only) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Entry-level residential | 300–360W | Budget systems, large roofs | $0.25–$0.40 |
| Mid-range mainstream | 370–420W | Most residential installs | $0.35–$0.55 |
| High-efficiency premium | 420–480W | Small roofs, shaded installs | $0.50–$0.75 |
| Premium/TOPCon or HJT | 480–580W | Space-constrained, max output | $0.65–$1.00+ |

Pricing above is panel-only cost, not installed cost. Installed residential systems run considerably higher once you add labor, inverters, racking, permitting, and the installer's margin. EnergySage's market data consistently puts the median installed cost somewhere around $2.50–$3.50 per watt before the federal tax credit, though local variation is real and significant.

## How to calculate how many panels you actually need

This is where most people get confused, because the math involves four different numbers: your monthly electricity consumption (in kWh), your location's peak sun hours, the wattage of the panel you're considering, and your system's overall efficiency losses.

Here's a worked example with clearly stated assumptions:

**Example:** A household in Phoenix, Arizona uses 1,200 kWh per month. Phoenix averages about 5.5 peak sun hours per day. The homeowner is considering 400W panels with a system derate factor of 0.85 (a reasonable mid-range assumption per NREL's PVWatts defaults).

Step 1: Convert monthly usage to daily. 1,200 kWh / 30 days = 40 kWh/day.

Step 2: Account for system losses. You need your panels to produce 40 kWh / 0.85 = 47.1 kWh/day before losses.

Step 3: Calculate required system size. 47.1 kWh / 5.5 peak sun hours = 8.56 kW required.

Step 4: Divide by panel wattage. 8,560W / 400W per panel = 21.4 panels, so 22 panels.

If those same panels were 320W instead, you'd need 27 panels for the same output. That's five more panels, more racking hardware, more roof space, and more labor. Same electricity production, meaningfully higher cost and complexity. This is why higher-wattage panels can actually be more cost-effective even though their per-panel price is higher.

If you want to run your own numbers before talking to a single installer, the [solar savings calculator](/solar-savings-calculator/) on this site lets you estimate production and savings based on your location and usage. Worth doing before you're on a sales call.

## When higher wattage isn't worth the premium

Don't let anyone talk you into the highest-wattage panels available if your situation doesn't call for it. If you have a large south-facing roof with no shading and plenty of space, mid-range 380–420W panels from a reputable manufacturer will give you an excellent system at better economics than premium 500W panels. The premium panels earn their cost when roof space is genuinely limited, when shading forces you to maximize watts per square foot, or when your permitting jurisdiction limits the number of panels rather than total system wattage.

Also worth knowing: wattage alone doesn't tell you about reliability or degradation. NREL research on long-term panel performance has found that most quality panels degrade at around 0.5% per year, meaning a 400W panel might produce about 390W in year 5 and around 350W in year 25. Some cheaper panels degrade faster. The spec to look for is the "power warranty," which reputable manufacturers guarantee at no less than 80–90% of rated output at 25 years. That warranty is worth reading, not just the headline number.

## What about system wattage vs. panel wattage?

These are related but different things. Panel wattage is the rated output of a single panel. System wattage is the total of all panels combined, what the industry calls "DC capacity." Your inverter then converts DC to AC power with some additional loss (typically 94–98% efficiency for a modern string inverter or microinverter).

A 10 kW DC system (say, 25 panels at 400W) will push through roughly 9.4–9.8 kW AC to your home's panel. Utility interconnection agreements and incentive programs often size limits around the AC output, so it's worth confirming with your installer which number they're quoting. Some installers quote DC, some quote AC, and conflating the two can make two competing bids look very different when they're actually proposing similar systems.
