My neighbor called me at 11 PM during a nor'easter two winters ago. Her house had solar panels, she'd been telling everyone about them for months, and now the grid was down and she was sitting in the dark. "I thought solar meant I'd have power," she said. I didn't have the heart to explain it right then, so I just brought over some candles.

Here's what nobody tells you clearly enough before you sign: solar panels alone do nothing during a grid outage. Zero. The inverter automatically shuts down within milliseconds of detecting a grid failure, and that's by design. It's a safety rule called anti-islanding, meant to protect utility workers from live wires. Without a battery system to island your home, your $25,000 solar installation becomes decorative during the one situation most people assume it handles.

This article is about the battery part. What actually happens when the grid goes down, which battery systems can keep your lights running (and your refrigerator, and your medical equipment), and whether the math makes sense for you. Because honestly, for some homeowners the answer is yes. For others, you'd be better off with a good generator. I've seen people spend $15,000 on battery storage who would've been fine with an $800 propane generator, and I've seen the reverse mistake too.

## Why your solar-plus-storage setup behaves differently than you expect

When a battery backup system is properly configured, here's what happens during an outage: the battery system detects the grid going down, disconnects your home from the grid (called "islanding"), and starts pulling from your battery bank and solar panels simultaneously. The transition can happen in under 20 milliseconds with newer systems like the Tesla Powerwall 3 or the Enphase IQ Battery 5P. Most sensitive electronics won't even notice.

But "solar-plus-storage" gets stretched to cover very different configurations. What you can actually run during an outage depends entirely on how the system is installed.

A partial-home backup protects a dedicated "critical loads" panel, typically 4-8 circuits the installer picks in advance. Your refrigerator, a few lights, maybe a phone charging outlet and the router. Cheaper to install because the battery doesn't have to carry the whole house. Whole-home backup does what it sounds like, but requires a larger battery bank and usually an automatic transfer switch rated for your home's full electrical load.

Here's something most people don't realize until they're buying: the inverter type matters as much as the battery itself. If you have a string inverter (one central inverter for all your panels, like older SolarEdge systems), backup capability gets more complicated and sometimes impossible without swapping equipment. Enphase microinverter systems with IQ8 microinverters can actually operate in "sunlight backup" mode without any battery at all, though only during daylight and at reduced capacity. That's genuinely unusual and worth knowing about if you're in early stages of choosing equipment.

## What a real battery system can and can't power

Let me give you actual numbers, because vague reassurances from a salesperson are useless.

The Tesla Powerwall 3 stores 13.5 kWh and outputs 11.5 kW continuous (with a 10-second surge capacity of 185A). The Enphase IQ Battery 5P stores 5 kWh per unit at 3.84 kW continuous power, so most installs stack two or three of them. The Franklin Electric apower 15.2 comes in at 15.2 kWh. Prices installed range roughly from $12,000 to $18,000 for a single Powerwall 3 depending on your market, installer, and what electrical work is required. According to EnergySage's 2024 market data, the median battery-only installation quote was around $1,200 per kWh installed, though that number varies significantly by region.

So what does 13.5 kWh actually buy you?

A typical refrigerator uses about 1-2 kWh per day. LED lighting for a few rooms runs maybe 0.3-0.5 kWh over a 12-hour night. Your router and a few phone chargers are negligible. A gas furnace with an electric igniter and blower fan uses roughly 0.5 kWh per hour of runtime. That combination, carefully managed, can stretch a single Powerwall through 24-36 hours of outage with no solar input at all.

Add solar recharging during the day and the math changes dramatically. A 10 kW solar array in decent sun can produce 35-50 kWh on a clear day. Even accounting for efficiency losses and the fact that you can't always store faster than the battery charges, a solar-plus-storage system can theoretically run indefinitely through a prolonged outage, as long as the sun cooperates and your loads stay reasonable.

What it won't handle without multiple batteries: a central air conditioner (3-5 kW when running), an electric vehicle charger, an electric stove or dryer, or an electric water heater. These loads drain a single Powerwall in hours. If whole-home backup with high-draw appliances matters to you, you're looking at two or three batteries minimum, which pushes installed cost past $25,000-$30,000. At that point, a lot of homeowners are better served by a hybrid approach, battery for essentials, generator for the heavy stuff.

## The 30% federal tax credit (and the clock ticking on it)

The federal Investment Tax Credit currently covers 30% of the total cost of a battery system when it's installed alongside solar, or when it charges primarily from solar. That number comes from the Inflation Reduction Act and is scheduled to step down after 2032, but there's enough political uncertainty right now that I wouldn't count on it staying exactly as written. For a $15,000 battery install, 30% back as a tax credit is $4,500, which meaningfully changes the real cost. It's a credit, not a deduction. You need actual federal tax liability to use it, so if you're retired and paying very little in federal taxes, you might only capture part of it (though it can be carried forward to future tax years).

Many states layer on additional incentives. California's SGIP (Self-Generation Incentive Program) has historically paid $150-$200 per kWh for battery installations in certain utility territories. Massachusetts has the Connected Solutions program. New York has the Con Edison and PSEG battery incentive programs. The U.S. Department of Energy maintains a database of state incentives worth checking before you buy, because these programs open and close and the amounts change.

I've seen homeowners skip this research and leave $3,000-$5,000 on the table. Don't do that.

## Choosing between systems: the honest version

The Tesla Powerwall 3 is the default recommendation for most installers because Tesla's brand recognition makes it easy to sell and the hardware is genuinely solid. The integrated inverter simplifies installation. But Tesla's customer support has gotten worse as the company's attention has scattered, and the Gateway unit that controls backup switching has had some reliability complaints in forums like r/SolarDIY.

Enphase IQ Battery 5P is my personal preference for most new installs where the homeowner is already using Enphase microinverters. The modular design means you can start with one 5 kWh unit and add more later. The app is excellent, the monitoring granular. The downside: at 3.84 kW per unit, you need at least two of them to run a well-pump or a larger AC unit during an outage.

The SunPower SunVault (which uses an LG Chem battery internally) and the Generac PWRcell are other options worth a quote on, especially if you're already working with installers who specialize in those brands.

One thing I'd tell any friend buying today: insist on knowing the chemistry. Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) batteries like the Powerwall 3 and most Enphase units have better thermal stability and a longer cycle life (typically 3,000-6,000 cycles vs. 1,000-2,000 for NMC chemistry). For a system you're hoping to run for 15+ years, that's not a trivial difference.
