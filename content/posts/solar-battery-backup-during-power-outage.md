My neighbor called me at 11 PM during a nor'easter two winters ago. Her house had solar panels, she'd been telling everyone about them for months, and now the grid was down and she was sitting in the dark. "I thought solar meant I'd have power," she said. I didn't have the heart to explain it right then, so I just brought over some candles.

Here's what nobody tells you clearly enough before you sign: solar panels alone do nothing during a grid outage. Zero. The inverter automatically shuts down within milliseconds of detecting a grid failure, and that's by design. It's a safety rule called anti-islanding, meant to protect utility workers from live wires. Without a battery system to island your home, your $25,000 solar installation becomes decorative during the one situation most people assume it handles.

This article is about the battery part. Specifically, what actually happens when the grid goes down, which battery systems can keep your lights (and refrigerator, and medical equipment) running, and whether the math makes sense for your situation. Because honestly, for some homeowners the answer is "yes, get a battery." For others, it's "you'd be better off with a good generator." I've seen people spend $15,000 on battery storage who would have been fine with a $800 propane generator, and I've seen the reverse mistake too.

## Why your solar-plus-storage setup behaves differently than you expect

When a battery backup system is properly configured, here's what actually happens during an outage: the battery system detects the grid going down, disconnects your home from the grid (this is called "islanding"), and then starts pulling from your battery bank and your solar panels simultaneously. The transition can happen in under 20 milliseconds with newer systems like the Tesla Powerwall 3 or the Enphase IQ Battery 5P. That's fast enough that most sensitive electronics won't even notice.

But "solar-plus-storage" is a phrase that gets stretched to cover very different configurations, and what you can actually run during an outage depends entirely on how the system is installed.

A partial-home backup setup protects a dedicated "critical loads" panel, typically 4-8 circuits the installer designates in advance. Your refrigerator, a few lights, maybe a phone charging outlet and the router. This is cheaper to install because the battery doesn't have to carry the whole house. A whole-home backup does exactly what it sounds like, but requires a larger battery bank and in most cases a automatic transfer switch rated for your home's full electrical load.

What most people don't realize until they're in the middle of buying something: the inverter type matters as much as the battery itself. If you have a string inverter (one central inverter for all your panels, like an older SolarEdge system), backup capability is more complicated to add and sometimes impossible without a full equipment swap. Enphase microinverter systems with IQ8 microinverters can actually operate in "sunlight backup" mode without any battery at all, though only during daylight and at reduced capacity. That's genuinely unusual functionality and worth knowing about if you're in early stages of choosing equipment.

Let me give you actual numbers, because vague reassurances from a salesperson are useless here.

The Tesla Powerwall 3 stores 13.5 kWh and outputs 11.5 kW continuous (with a 10-second surge capacity of 185A). The Enphase IQ Battery 5P stores 5 kWh per unit at 3.84 kW continuous power, so most installs stack two or three of them. The Franklin Electric apower (aPower) 15.2 comes in at 15.2 kWh. Prices installed range roughly from $12,000 to $18,000 for a single Powerwall 3 depending on your market, installer, and what electrical work is required. According to EnergySage's market data, the median battery-only installation quote in 2024 was around $1,200 per kWh installed, though that number varies significantly by region.

So what does 13.5 kWh actually buy you?

A typical refrigerator uses about 1-2 kWh per day. LED lighting for a few rooms, call it 0.3-0.5 kWh over a 12-hour night. Your router and a few phone chargers, negligible. A gas furnace with an electric igniter and blower fan, roughly 0.5 kWh per hour of runtime. That combination, carefully managed, can stretch a single Powerwall through 24-36 hours of outage with no solar input at all.

Add solar recharging during the day and the math changes dramatically. A 10 kW solar array in a decent sun region can produce 35-50 kWh on a clear day. Even accounting for efficiency losses and the fact that you can't always store faster than the battery charges, a solar-plus-storage system can theoretically run indefinitely through a prolonged outage, as long as the sun cooperates and your loads stay reasonable.

What it won't handle, or at least won't handle without multiple batteries: a central air conditioner (3-5 kW when running), an electric vehicle charger, an electric stove or dryer, or an electric water heater. These loads will drain a single Powerwall in hours. If whole-home backup with high-draw appliances matters to you, you're looking at two or three batteries minimum, which pushes installed cost past $25,000-$30,000. At that point, a lot of homeowners are better served by a hybrid approach: battery for essentials, generator for the heavy stuff.

## The 26% federal tax credit (and the clock ticking on it)

The federal Investment Tax Credit currently covers 30% of the total cost of a battery system when it's installed alongside solar, or when it charges primarily from solar. That number is baked into the Inflation Reduction Act and is scheduled to step down after 2032, but there's enough political uncertainty right now that I wouldn't count on it staying exactly as written. For a $15,000 battery install, 30% back as a tax credit is $4,500, which meaningfully changes the real cost. Note that it's a credit, not a deduction. You need actual federal tax liability to use it, so if you're retired and paying very little in federal taxes, you might only capture part of it (though it can be carried forward to future tax years).

Many states layer on additional incentives. California's SGIP (Self-Generation Incentive Program) has historically paid $150-$200 per kWh for battery installations in certain utility territories. Massachusetts has the Connected Solutions program. New York has the Con Edison and PSEG battery incentive programs. The U.S. Department of Energy maintains a database of state incentives worth checking before you buy, because these programs open and close and the amounts change.

I've seen homeowners skip this research and leave $3,000-$5,000 on the table. Don't do that.

## Choosing between systems: the honest version

The Tesla Powerwall 3 is the default recommendation for most installers because Tesla's brand recognition makes it easy to sell and the hardware is genuinely solid. The integrated inverter (the Powerwall 3 has a built-in solar inverter) simplifies installation. But Tesla's customer support has gotten worse as the company's attention has scattered, and the Gateway unit that controls backup switching has had some reliability complaints in forums like r/SolarDIY.

Enphase IQ Battery 5P is my personal preference for most new installs where the homeowner is already using Enphase microinverters. The modular design means you can start with one 5 kWh unit and add more later. The app is excellent, the monitoring granular. The downside: at 3.84 kW per unit, you need at least two of them to run a well-pump or a larger AC unit during an outage.

The SunPower SunVault (which uses an LG Chem battery internally) and the Generac PWRcell are other options worth a quote on, especially if you're already working with installers who specialize in those brands.

One thing I'd tell any friend buying today: insist on knowing the chemistry. Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) batteries like the Powerwall 3 and most Enphase units have better thermal stability and a longer cycle life (typically 3,000-6,000 cycles vs. 1,000-2,000 for NMC chemistry). For a system you're hoping to run for 15+ years, that's not a trivial difference.

For monitoring your system's performance during and after an outage, a whole-home energy monitor like the Emporia Vue Gen 2 (available on Amazon, and yes, this site may earn a commission) can show you exactly which circuits are drawing power, which helps you manage loads intelligently when you're running on stored energy.

## FAQ

### Will my solar panels work during a power outage without a battery?

No. Without battery storage, your solar panels automatically shut off when the grid goes down. This is a mandatory safety feature called anti-islanding, required by utilities to protect line workers. The only exception is Enphase IQ8 microinverters in "sunlight backup" mode, which allows limited daytime-only power without a battery, but that's a specific equipment configuration, not a general solar feature.

### How long will a single Tesla Powerwall last during an outage?

It depends heavily on what you're running. With conservative usage (refrigerator, lights, router, phone charging) and no solar recharging, a single Powerwall 3's 13.5 kWh typically lasts 24-36 hours. If you're also recharging from solar panels during daylight, an outage of several days is manageable as long as you avoid high-draw appliances like AC, electric dryers, or EV chargers.

### Does a solar battery qualify for the federal tax credit?

Yes, if it's charged primarily by solar. The federal Investment Tax Credit covers 30% of installed battery costs when the system is connected to solar panels. Standalone batteries (not connected to solar) also now qualify under the Inflation Reduction Act, as long as they're charged at least 70% from solar energy. The credit applies to your federal income tax liability, so you need to owe taxes to fully benefit.

### Is a whole-home battery backup worth it compared to a generator?

Honestly, it depends on your outage history and how much you value a seamless, fuel-free experience. A propane or natural gas standby generator from Generac or Kohler can power your whole home for days at a fraction of the cost, typically $5,000-$12,000 installed. Batteries win on silence, zero maintenance, no fuel storage, and the ability to charge from solar. If you live somewhere with frequent multi-day outages, a hybrid setup (battery for essentials, generator for backup) often makes the most practical sense.

### Can I add a battery to my existing solar system?

Sometimes yes, sometimes it's complicated. If you have an Enphase microinverter system, adding an IQ Battery is relatively straightforward. If you have an older string inverter, you may need a hybrid inverter retrofit, which can add $2,000-$5,000 to the project. Get quotes from at least two installers and ask specifically whether your existing inverter is compatible, not just whether a battery can be "added."
