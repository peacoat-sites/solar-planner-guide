Most homeowners going solar save somewhere between $100 and $150 per month in the first year, but that range is almost meaningless without context. Your actual number depends on how much electricity you use, what your utility charges per kilowatt-hour, how much sun your roof gets, and what size system you install. Those four variables can push your monthly savings anywhere from $50 to over $300.

The good news: this is not a guessing game. You can build a reasonable estimate in about 20 minutes using your utility bills and a few public tools. What follows is how to do that, plus the honest version of what most installers don't tell you.

## What Actually Drives Your Monthly Savings

Start with your utility rate. The national average residential electricity rate is around 16–17 cents per kilowatt-hour, but that number hides a lot. Connecticut and Massachusetts customers regularly pay 25–30 cents. California's tiered rates can exceed 35 cents in upper tiers. If you're in one of those states, solar math looks fantastic. If you're in Louisiana or Washington state, where rates sit closer to 9–11 cents, the numbers are tighter and payback takes longer.

Your consumption matters just as much. A 2,000 sq ft home in Phoenix running central AC might use 1,500–2,000 kWh per month in summer. That same-sized home in Seattle might use 700 kWh. Solar panels produce power roughly proportional to sun hours, and [NREL's PVWatts calculator](https://www.nrel.gov/) lets you plug in your zip code, system size, and roof angle to get a realistic annual production estimate. It's free, and it's what professional installers use.

Then there's net metering. Under a full retail [net metering policy](/net-metering-policy-by-state/), every kWh you send to the grid in a sunny afternoon offsets a kWh you pull back at night, dollar for dollar. That's the ideal scenario. But a growing number of utilities have shifted to "net billing" or reduced export rates, meaning you get paid 3–8 cents for power you export but pay 16+ cents for power you import. That gap eats directly into your savings. California's NEM 3.0 policy, which took effect in 2023, is the most prominent example: new solar customers in that state now receive much lower export credits, making battery storage far more attractive. Check your utility's current interconnection tariff before trusting any installer's savings estimate.

## Running the Numbers: A Worked Example

**Example:** Assume a homeowner in Atlanta, Georgia. Monthly electricity use: 1,100 kWh. Current utility rate: $0.14/kWh. Monthly bill before solar: roughly $154.

Atlanta gets approximately 4.5 peak sun hours per day, per NREL data. A 7 kW system there produces about 7,000 watts × 4.5 hours × 365 days × 0.80 (efficiency/derate factor) = approximately 9,198 kWh per year, or about 766 kWh per month on average.

That 766 kWh offsets 766 × $0.14 = $107 per month, assuming full retail net metering credit. The homeowner still draws about 334 kWh from the grid monthly, paying around $47. Net monthly savings: roughly $107. Annual savings: around $1,284.

That 7 kW system costs roughly $21,000 before incentives at today's installed prices. The 30% federal Investment Tax Credit (ITC) brings that to $14,700. If Georgia has no additional state incentive (it largely doesn't), payback period is $14,700 / $1,284 = 11.4 years. Not spectacular, but serviceable given panel warranties of 25 years.

Change the utility rate to $0.25/kWh (think New England), and the same usage and system suddenly saves $191/month. Payback drops to under 7 years.

## Who Should Think Twice

Solar saves money for most homeowners who own their home, have adequate sun exposure (no heavy shading from trees or buildings), and plan to stay put for at least 7–10 years. But it's not the right call for everyone.

If you're renting, you don't control the roof. If your roof needs replacement within 5 years, the cost of removing and reinstalling panels likely erodes your early savings. If your utility has already moved to a poor net billing structure and you're not willing to add a battery, your savings estimate from an installer quoting full retail rates is wrong. Some utilities have also added fixed monthly charges for solar customers (sometimes called "grid access fees"), which can run $10–$30/month and directly reduce net savings.

Leases and PPAs are a separate animal. You don't own the system, you don't get the tax credit, and your savings are smaller (typically 10–25% off your current bill, not 70–100%). If an installer is pushing a lease hard without explaining that tradeoff, that's a flag.

## Getting an Accurate Personal Estimate

The most reliable path: pull 12 months of utility bills, note your total kWh consumed (not just dollars), and use NREL's PVWatts to model a system sized to cover 90–95% of that usage. Then get at minimum three quotes through a comparison marketplace and cross-check the production estimates across quotes. If one installer's annual production estimate is 20% higher than the others, ask why.

A home energy monitor can also help you find usage you can cut before sizing a solar system. Reducing consumption first means buying a smaller (cheaper) system to cover the same percentage of your bill. Devices like the [Emporia Vue 2 (available on Amazon)](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Emporia+Vue+2+energy+monitor) give you circuit-level data and typically run under $100. *(Note: this site may earn a commission on purchases.)*

One step most people skip: check your utility's interconnection queue wait time before signing anything. In some regions, the wait to get a system approved and activated is running 3–6 months or longer, which delays the point at which savings actually start.
