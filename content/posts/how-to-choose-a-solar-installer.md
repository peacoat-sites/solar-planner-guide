## Why Your Choice of Installer Matters More Than Your Choice of Panels

The panels get all the attention. Monocrystalline vs. polycrystalline, efficiency ratings, degradation curves. Meanwhile, the installer is the variable that actually determines whether your system performs, gets permitted correctly, passes inspection, and comes with warranty support that exists five years from now.

Consider: a Tier 1 panel installed badly underperforms a mid-grade panel installed well. Poor wiring connections, incorrect azimuth angles, inadequate conduit sealing -- these aren't hypotheticals. I've seen brand-new systems underperform by 15-20% because of sloppy installation that took years to diagnose.

The Solar Energy Industries Association (SEIA) tracks installer data and has documented that installation quality and post-installation service are among the top drivers of customer complaints. The hardware is commoditized. The labor isn't.

Do this screening work before you talk to anyone's sales team. It takes two hours and will save you from a lot of noise.

**Check their license status.** Every state has a contractor licensing board with a public lookup tool. Verify the company holds a valid electrical contractor's license in your state, not just a general contractor's license. Solar installation is electrical work. Some states require a specific solar contractor classification. Look it up.

**Verify NABCEP certification.** The North American Board of Certified Energy Practitioners is the gold standard credential in this industry. Look for installers who employ NABCEP-certified PV Installation Professionals. This isn't a guarantee of quality, but it's a meaningful signal. Any company without at least one NABCEP-certified person on staff is worth scrutinizing harder.

**Check their track record on the Better Business Bureau and Google Reviews -- but read critically.** A 4.8-star average with 12 reviews means almost nothing. A 4.3-star average with 340 reviews is much more informative. Focus on one-star reviews and how the company responds. Complaints about poor communication and post-installation service problems are red flags. Complaints about permit delays are less concerning because permitting is often outside an installer's control.

**How long have they been in business?** The solar industry has had multiple boom-and-bust cycles. Companies that started after 2019 have never operated through a real contraction. That's not disqualifying, but a company with 10+ years of local operation has survived at least one downturn. That matters for your 25-year warranty to mean anything.

**Are they local or a national company with local crews?** Both models can work. National companies like Sunrun or SunPower have scale and financial stability. Local installers often have faster response times and more invested reputations. The risk with national companies is that your installation gets handed to a subcontractor you never vetted. Ask directly: who physically installs the system? Are they employees or subcontractors?

## How to Get Quotes That Are Actually Comparable

Most homeowners get one quote, maybe two. According to EnergySage's market data, homeowners who get at least three quotes save an average of 20% compared to those who get one. That's thousands of dollars for a few hours of additional effort.

Getting three comparable quotes requires a little structure, otherwise you're comparing apples to mangoes.

**Request quotes in writing that specify:**

- System size in kilowatts (kW) DC
- Panel brand, model, and rated wattage
- Inverter brand and type (string inverter, microinverters, or power optimizers with a string inverter)
- Estimated annual production in kilowatt-hours (kWh)
- Warranty terms: workmanship warranty length, panel performance warranty, inverter warranty
- Timeline from contract signing to Permission to Operate (PTO)
- What's included in the price: permits, utility interconnection fees, inspection

When you have three quotes in this format, comparison becomes straightforward. If a quote is missing any of these items, that's a signal about how they operate.

**A note on price per watt:** The industry average installed cost in the U.S. runs around $2.95 per watt before the federal tax credit, according to recent EnergySage data. A quote below $2.50/watt should prompt questions about what's being cut. A quote above $3.50/watt should come with a clear explanation of what's commanding the premium.

## The Equipment Conversation: What Installers Won't Volunteer

Most installers will propose whatever equipment maximizes their margin while staying competitive. That's not malicious. It's business. But you should know what trade-offs are being made.

**Inverters matter as much as panels, possibly more.** A string inverter is the cheapest option and works fine on a simple south-facing roof with no shading. If your roof has any shading from trees, dormers, or chimneys, or faces multiple directions, microinverters or power optimizers with a DC-optimized string inverter will meaningfully outperform. Enphase makes the dominant microinverter; SolarEdge and APsystems are the main competitors in power optimizers.

Ask the installer to show you a shading analysis. Tools like Aurora or Helioscope generate this automatically. If they can't show you one, they're either not doing the analysis or don't want you to see the results.

**Panel warranties have two components.** The product warranty covers defects (typically 12-25 years). The performance warranty guarantees the panel will still produce at least 80-90% of its rated output after 25 years. Most reputable Tier 1 manufacturers now offer 25-year comprehensive warranties. If someone is quoting you panels with a 10-year product warranty, ask why.

**Monitoring is not optional.** You need to be able to see whether your system is producing what it was promised. Most modern inverters include monitoring through a manufacturer app (Enphase Enlighten, SolarEdge monitoring portal). Make sure this is included and working before you sign off on final installation. For whole-home energy visibility, a home energy monitor like the [Emporia Vue Energy Monitor](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=emporia+vue+energy+monitor) (Amazon, and the site may earn a commission) can show you exactly how much solar production you're consuming vs. exporting to the grid, which is genuinely useful data.

## Red Flags That Should End a Conversation

I'll be direct about what should make you walk away.

**High-pressure closing tactics.** "This price is only good today" is a manipulation technique. Solar equipment prices don't fluctuate daily. The federal investment tax credit (ITC) is currently 30% and doesn't have an expiration date forcing a same-day decision. Any installer who creates artificial urgency is telling you something about how they operate.

**Promises about utility rates.** Some sales pitches include projections showing your electricity bill will be $0 forever. Utility rates do rise over time, historically around 2-3% per year nationally. But an installer who promises specific savings based on specific future utility rate increases is making up numbers. Projections should show a range of scenarios.

**Unclear or verbal-only warranty terms.** Your workmanship warranty needs to be in writing in the contract. Verbal promises about "lifetime support" are worthless. Ask specifically: if this company goes out of business, what happens to my warranty? A good answer involves manufacturer warranties that are independent of the installer.

**No site visit before the quote.** A legitimate installer should send a technician or use satellite imagery tools (and sometimes both) to evaluate your roof condition, attic space, electrical panel capacity, and shading. A quote generated entirely from a phone call without any site assessment will contain errors that surface later.

The U.S. Department of Energy's homeowner guide to going solar specifically calls out the importance of verifying contractor credentials and getting multiple bids before signing, which is sound advice that's ignored more often than it should be.

## Step-by-Step: Vetting an Installer Before You Sign

Here's the actual sequence, in order.

1. **Run three candidates through the license and NABCEP check.** Eliminate anyone who fails.
2. **Pull their BBB file and read every negative review.** Look for patterns, not isolated incidents.
3. **Request written quotes from all three** using the spec list above. Give each a deadline of 10 days.
4. **Ask each installer: who physically does the installation, and are they employees or subcontractors?** Get a name if possible.
5. **Ask for two or three local customer references.** Then actually call them. Ask specifically about the inspection process, any post-installation issues, and response time when they had questions.
6. **Compare workmanship warranty length.** Industry standard is 10 years. Some offer 25. This is a meaningful differentiator.
7. **Check financial stability.** For large national companies, public financial filings are available. For local companies, how long they've been in business and whether they own their equipment vs. lease it to you matters.
8. **Negotiate.** Installers have margin. If you have competing quotes, use them. Even a 5-10% reduction is $1,000-$2,000 on a typical residential system.
