## Yes, You Almost Certainly Need Permits

There is no federal law that says you must pull a permit for solar. What there is, instead, is a patchwork of state and local codes that almost universally require it. The National [Renewable Energy](/srec-solar-renewable-energy-credit/) Laboratory (NREL) has tracked solar permitting across hundreds of jurisdictions and consistently found that unpermitted residential solar installations are the exception, not the rule, and that when homeowners skip permits, they typically discover the problem at resale, during a homeowner's insurance claim, or both.

The specific permits you'll need depend on your jurisdiction, but the two most common are a building permit (covering structural modifications to your roof) and an electrical permit (covering the wiring, inverter, and connection to your panel). Some jurisdictions roll these into a single "solar permit." Others also require a separate [utility interconnection](/utility-interconnection-process-solar/) application before they'll allow permission to operate, which is different from a building permit but equally non-negotiable.

A reader named Greg emailed me last spring after he'd had solar installed in 2022 without proper permits because his installer (a small two-person shop) told him "permits weren't necessary for roof-mounted systems in his county." Three years later, Greg tried to refinance and his lender's appraiser flagged the unpermitted work. He ended up having to retroactively permit the system, which meant an inspection of work that had already been done and a $940 fine from his county. Don't be Greg.

Here's where I'll give you the real walkthrough, because the standard installer pitch of "don't worry, we handle everything" glosses over some details worth knowing.

Most reputable installers do submit permits on your behalf. What they won't tell you is that you, as the property owner, are the permit applicant of record in most states. The installer acts as your agent. If they pull the permit incorrectly, or if work begins before approval comes through (a pressure tactic I've seen from a handful of high-volume installers), that liability falls on you.

The process generally goes like this:

1. Your installer designs the system and prepares permit documents: a site plan, electrical diagrams, a structural analysis (sometimes a wet-stamped engineer's letter if your roof is older or non-standard), and equipment spec sheets.
2. They submit to your local Authority Having Jurisdiction, usually your city or county building department.
3. The department reviews, sometimes requests corrections or additional documentation, then issues the permit.
4. Installation happens under the permit.
5. An inspector visits to review the work, typically checking both the structural attachment points and the electrical connections.
6. Your utility receives a Permission to Operate (PTO) letter, which is separate from the building permit, and you're cleared to turn the system on.

Step 6 is the one that trips people up. A lot of homeowners think the system is "done" when installation finishes. The panels are on the roof, the inverter is humming, and the installer is gone. But you cannot legally operate a grid-tied system without PTO from your utility. I've talked to homeowners who waited 3 to 8 weeks after installation for their utility to issue PTO. During that time, the system sat dormant. This is normal, but no one warns you about it.

That SolarApp+ row is worth calling out. It's a permit automation platform developed with Department of Energy support, and as of August 2026, a growing number of jurisdictions have adopted it. If your installer mentions that your city uses SolarApp+, that's genuinely good news: permit turnaround can drop to 24 hours in some cases. The Solar Energy Industries Association (SEIA) has been pushing hard for broader adoption, with mixed results depending on state.

The cost of permits is real but relatively minor in the context of a full solar installation. On a $18,000 system, paying $350 in permit fees is noise. What isn't noise is a 10-week delay if your jurisdiction is backlogged, because that's 10 weeks you're not producing energy and offsetting your electricity bill.

## What Your Installer Should Be Doing (And What to Ask If You're Not Sure)

I once reviewed a contract for a reader in New Jersey where the installer had included a blanket clause saying the homeowner was responsible for permit delays. That's not inherently dishonest, but it obscures something worth knowing: good installers build jurisdiction-specific timelines into their project schedules. If an installer tells you installation will start "within two to three weeks of signing," and you're in a jurisdiction known for 6-week permit reviews, that's a red flag.

Ask your installer directly: Which permits are required in my jurisdiction? Have you pulled permits there before? What's a realistic timeline from signing to Permission to Operate? If they can't answer the third question with any specificity, that's concerning.

Also worth asking: whether your HOA has a separate approval process. This is totally separate from city permits, and some HOAs have tried to block or restrict solar, though many states now have laws limiting HOA authority over solar installations. California, Florida, and Texas, among others, have strong solar access laws. If you're in a state without them, HOA pushback can add weeks or kill a project entirely.
