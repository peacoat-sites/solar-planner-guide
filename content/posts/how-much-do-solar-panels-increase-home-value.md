The average American home gains about $15,000 in resale value after a solar installation. That number gets quoted constantly by solar salespeople, and I'll be honest: it's not wrong, but it's also not the whole story. That figure comes from a 2019 Zillow analysis, and when you actually look at what's behind it, the picture gets both more interesting and more complicated than any installer is going to tell you sitting at your kitchen table.

I've spent a lot of time with homeowners trying to figure out whether solar is a smart financial move, and the resale value question is one of the trickiest because the answer genuinely varies by so much depending on where you live, how you bought the system, and what the local real estate market is doing. Let me walk you through what the data actually says.

## The Core Number, and Why It's Slippery

The most-cited research on solar home value comes from a Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory study (updated several times, most recently drawing on sales data through 2020) that found buyers paid a premium of roughly $4 per watt of installed solar capacity. On a typical 6-kilowatt system, that's about $24,000. Zillow's separate analysis found solar homes sold for 4.1% more on average. On a $400,000 home, that's $16,400.

So right away you have two credible sources giving you numbers in the same ballpark but not identical. The research here isn't exactly mixed, but it's not as clean as "solar adds $X to your home." The premium depends on:

- Your electricity rates (high-rate states like California, Massachusetts, and Connecticut show bigger premiums)
- Whether the system is owned outright or under a lease or PPA
- How new the system is and how much productive life it has left
- The local real estate market's familiarity with solar

That last one surprises people. In markets where solar is everywhere, appraisers and buyers understand the value. In markets where solar is still relatively unusual, appraisers sometimes struggle to find "comps" with solar, which can lead them to assign little or no value to the system at all.

I cannot stress this enough: a leased solar system or one under a power purchase agreement (PPA) does not add value to your home the way an owned system does. It can actually complicate or slow a sale.

When you lease panels, you don't own them. The solar company does. When you go to sell your house, the buyer has to either take over the lease payments or you have to buy out the lease, which can cost anywhere from $10,000 to $30,000 depending on how many years are left. I've talked to real estate agents who've watched deals fall apart over leased solar, because buyers either don't want to assume the contract or don't qualify for the lease transfer.

Owned systems are a completely different story. According to [EnergySage's market data](https://news.energysage.com/), the typical homeowner who purchases their system outright (either with cash or a solar loan that they've paid off) captures that $4/watt premium far more reliably than someone transferring a third-party-owned system.

If you're considering solar and you're not planning to stay in the house for 20+ years, this distinction should be a major factor in how you finance it.

## How Location Warps the Math

Here's what surprised me the most when I started digging into the geographic data: the premium isn't evenly distributed across the country at all.

A Fannie Mae analysis and supporting work from [the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL)](https://www.nrel.gov/) both found that states with high electricity rates tend to show the largest solar premiums. This makes intuitive sense. A solar system in Massachusetts, where residential electricity costs around 25 cents per kilowatt-hour, is producing more annual savings than the identical system in Louisiana, where you're paying closer to 11 cents. Buyers understand this, at least implicitly. The annual value of the offset electricity is higher, so they'll pay more to own it.

In California, some studies have found premiums exceeding $5 or even $6 per watt in certain markets. In the Midwest and South, the premium can fall to $2 or $3 per watt, or sometimes less.

What this means practically: don't take the $15,000 national average and assume it applies to your home in Des Moines or Baton Rouge. It probably doesn't.

## The Appraisal Problem Nobody Talks About

Even when buyers are willing to pay more for a solar home, that premium has to survive the appraisal process. And this is where things can quietly fall apart.

Residential appraisers are supposed to use an "income approach" to value income-producing features like solar, calculating the present value of future electricity savings. Some appraisers do this correctly. A lot don't, especially in markets where solar is still relatively rare and there aren't enough recent "solar comps" to work from.

A reader emailed me last spring about exactly this situation. She'd accepted an offer on her house at $18,000 above her original ask, largely because the buyers specifically wanted the solar. Then the appraisal came in without assigning any value to the system. The deal nearly collapsed. They had to renegotiate.

This is a real risk. If you're selling a solar home, it's worth finding a real estate agent with direct experience selling solar properties, and potentially requesting an appraiser with PV [photovoltaic] valuation training. The Appraisal Institute has a specific credential for this (the "Green and Energy Efficient Addendum" process), though not every appraiser uses it.

## Does System Age Matter?

Yes, a lot. A brand-new 8kW system with 25 years of warranty coverage left is worth considerably more than a 15-year-old 4kW system with degraded panels and an inverter that might need replacing.

Solar panels lose roughly 0.5% of output per year, per standard industry estimates. A 10-year-old system is producing about 95% of what it did when new, which isn't catastrophic. But an older system also means older inverters (string inverters typically last 10-15 years, so you may be looking at a $1,000 to $2,000 replacement cost soon), and older monitoring equipment.

If you're buying a home with existing solar, ask for the monitoring data. Most modern systems come with monitoring apps that show historical production. If the seller can't produce that data, that's a yellow flag. A good home energy monitor like the [Emporia Vue](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Emporia+Vue+energy+monitor&tag=yourtag-20) *(note: this site may earn a commission on purchases)* can help you understand what an existing system is actually producing before you commit.

## What Buyers Actually Pay For

Here's my honest take after looking at all of this: buyers don't pay a premium for solar as a technology. They pay a premium for electricity cost certainty.

The most compelling selling point isn't "we have panels on the roof." It's "our average electric bill was $38 a month last year, here are the utility statements." That's the number that makes buyers eyes light up. A $400 annual electricity bill vs. a $2,400 one is a concrete, tangible thing that maps directly onto monthly mortgage payment math.

This means that how you document and present your system's production history matters more than most sellers realize. Keep your utility bills. Export your monitoring data. Have your annual production reports ready. If your system has been producing, prove it. That documentation is worth real money.

## FAQ

### Does solar increase property taxes on my home?

In most states, no. More than 35 states have property tax exemptions specifically for solar installations, meaning the added value from solar won't increase your annual property tax bill. California, Texas, and Florida all have these exemptions. Always verify with your specific county assessor's office, because exemptions can vary at the local level.

### How much value does solar add per watt?

The most widely cited figure, from Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's long-running "Tracking the Sun" research, is approximately $4 per watt of installed capacity. That means a 6kW system could add around $24,000 in resale value, though the actual premium varies significantly by state, electricity rates, and local market conditions.

### Do solar panels hurt home sales or make homes harder to sell?

Owned solar systems generally don't hurt sales and often help. Leased systems are a more complicated story and have been known to slow or complicate transactions, because the buyer needs to assume the lease or the seller must buy it out. In markets with strong solar familiarity, owned systems typically sell faster, not slower.

### Will an appraiser give full value to my solar panels?

Not always. Appraiser familiarity with solar valuation varies widely, and in markets without many recent solar comp sales, appraisers sometimes assign little or no value to a system. Working with an agent experienced in solar home sales and requesting a PV-knowledgeable appraiser can help close that gap.

### Does a solar system need to be paid off to add resale value?

If you took out a solar loan and it's still outstanding, the lien on the system can complicate the title transfer at sale. It doesn't eliminate the value, but you'll generally need to pay off the loan at closing, similar to any other secured debt. Factor this into your equity calculations before listing.

The $15,000 figure isn't a myth, but it's a national average masking a wide range of real outcomes. In San Jose it might be $30,000. In a rural Midwest market with low electricity rates and appraisers who've never valued a solar system, it might be close to zero. The honest answer is that solar's value to your home is real and often substantial, especially if you own the system outright, you're in a high-rate state, and you have the production records to back it up. Get those three things right and the premium is more than just a talking point.
