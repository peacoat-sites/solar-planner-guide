If you've been sitting on the fence about going solar in California, I'll be honest: the window you missed in 2022 was genuinely great, and what replaced it is genuinely worse. That's not a scare tactic. It's just the math.

NEM 3.0, officially called Net Billing Tariff (NBT), went into effect on April 15, 2023 for California's three big investor-owned utilities: PG&E, SCE, and SDG&E. It replaced NEM 2.0, the policy that made California the country's largest solar market for over a decade. And the change is substantial enough that it's worth understanding before you sign a single installer quote.

## What NEM 2.0 Actually Was (And Why Losing It Stings)

| Metric | NEM 2.0 | NEM 3.0 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Export Credit Rate | ~$0.40-$0.50/kWh (summer, typical SDG&E) | $0.04-$0.08/kWh (midday); $0.15-$0.30/kWh (evening peak) |
| Payback Period | 5-7 years | 10-14 years |
| Export Credit Change | Baseline | ~75% reduction |
| Self-Consumed Power Value | ~Retail rate | Full retail rate ($0.35-$0.50/kWh) |
| Policy Focus | High export credits | Battery storage & load shifting |

Under NEM 2.0, the deal was simple and good. You sent excess solar electricity to the grid, and the utility credited you at roughly the retail rate you'd otherwise pay for that power. On a typical SDG&E plan, that meant earning somewhere around $0.40 to $0.50 per kWh for surplus solar in summer. The payback period on a California solar system hovered around 5 to 7 years for most households, and the 25-year ROI made solar one of the better investments a homeowner could make, full stop.

What surprised me when I started pulling actual data from California Public Utilities Commission filings was how aggressively the new rates cut those credits. Under NEM 3.0, export compensation is tied to what the CPUC calls the "Avoided Cost Calculator," which values your exported power at essentially the wholesale cost the utility pays for energy at a given hour. That works out to something like $0.04 to $0.08 per kWh during midday hours when the grid is already flooded with solar, rising to $0.15 to $0.30 per kWh during evening peak hours (roughly 4 to 9 PM). The CPUC's own analysis estimated average export credits would drop about 75% compared to NEM 2.0.

Seventy-five percent. That's not a tweak. That's a restructuring.

The pitch from the CPUC is that NEM 2.0 was unfair to non-solar ratepayers who subsidized solar owners' high credits. There's genuine merit to that argument. But the cure has some painful side effects for anyone going solar today.

## The Real Impact on Your Payback Period

Here's where the numbers get concrete and where most installer sales pitches go a little soft.

NREL modeling published after the NEM 3.0 rollout estimated that [the payback period for a solar-only system in California could stretch to 10 to 14 years](https://www.nrel.gov/) under NEM 3.0, compared to the 5 to 7 year range most NEM 2.0 customers saw. That's a meaningful change. A 10-year payback on a $25,000 system is a different financial proposition than a 6-year payback.

The reason the math shifts so dramatically comes down to when you use power. Under NEM 2.0, it didn't matter much whether you used solar electricity directly or exported it; you got close to the same value either way. Under NEM 3.0, self-consumption is king. Electricity you use directly from your panels is worth full retail rates, which can run $0.35 to $0.50 per kWh for PG&E customers on tiered rates. Electricity you export at noon? Maybe $0.05. The difference is enormous, and it completely changes how you should think about system sizing, battery storage, and your own daily habits.

A reader emailed me a few months ago after getting three installer bids. All three proposed essentially the same system they would have sold in 2021: panels sized to offset 100% of annual usage, no battery, no mention of NEM 3.0 rate differences. That's not fraud, but it's not doing right by the customer either.

## Why Batteries Changed from "Nice to Have" to Actually Doing Math

I'll be honest: before NEM 3.0, I told most California homeowners they didn't need a battery unless they were in a wildfire zone with frequent outages. The economics just weren't there.

That calculus has flipped.

The time-of-use arbitrage under NEM 3.0 is real. If you have a battery like a Tesla Powerwall 3 (currently around $11,500 installed), you can store solar energy produced at midday when export rates are terrible, then discharge during the evening peak when retail rates hit their highest point. On PG&E's E-TOUD rate schedule, the peak tier runs $0.47 to $0.56 per kWh in summer. You're charging your battery with $0.05/kWh solar and displacing $0.52/kWh grid power. That's a genuinely good trade.

The CPUC actually designed NEM 3.0 with this in mind. Solar plus storage systems get modestly better export rates in the early filing period, and the policy explicitly favors systems that shift load rather than dump solar onto the grid at noon. Whether you love or hate the policy as a matter of principle, the financial signal is clear: go solar plus storage, and do it thoughtfully.

To make the most of this setup, you'll want solid monitoring. I've seen homeowners get real value from a home energy monitor like the [Emporia Vue](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Emporia+Vue+energy+monitor) (around $60 on Amazon, affiliate link), which lets you see exactly which appliances are consuming power and when. That visibility helps you shift dishwashers, EV charging, and pool pumps to solar production hours. It matters a lot more now than it did under NEM 2.0.

## What a Smart NEM 3.0 System Actually Looks Like

Given all this, what should a California homeowner actually do?

The biggest shift in system design: don't overbuild. Under NEM 2.0, sizing a system to 110% or 120% of annual usage made sense because you'd get near-retail credit for everything you exported. Under NEM 3.0, excess export is nearly worthless. A system that exports huge amounts of midday power is leaving money on the table. You want to size panels to match your self-consumption profile, then use a battery to capture what you can't use immediately.

If you have an EV, a pool, or you work from home, you're actually in a better position than the average NEM 3.0 customer because you have more daytime load to consume solar directly. [The U.S. Department of Energy's homeowner guide](https://www.energy.gov/eere/solar/homeowners-guide-going-solar) has good material on load analysis, and it's worth doing before you sit down with any installer.

For panels, the efficiency question matters more under NEM 3.0 because every kWh you generate and use is worth more than anything you export. Panels like the Panasonic EverVolt or REC Alpha Pure-R, both in the 22-23% efficiency range, can squeeze more production out of limited roof space compared to standard 20% panels. The price premium is usually worth it if your roof is small or partially shaded.

One thing installers sometimes gloss over: the interconnection timeline. As of late 2024, PG&E and SDG&E interconnection queues have stretched to 4 to 9 months in many areas. That's time you're not producing power and not earning credits. Factor it into your decision, especially if you're planning around a federal tax credit deadline.

## The 30% Federal Tax Credit Still Applies, and It's Real Money

The Inflation Reduction Act's residential clean energy credit gives you 30% of total system cost back as a direct tax credit. On a $30,000 solar plus storage system, that's $9,000 off your federal tax bill. That credit applies to battery storage even if it's added without new panels starting in 2023.

This doesn't change the NEM 3.0 math, but it meaningfully changes the absolute numbers. A $30,000 system becomes a $21,000 out-of-pocket system if you have sufficient federal tax liability to absorb the credit. That improved payback period enough that plenty of NEM 3.0 systems still pencil out, particularly when you factor in California's generally rising utility rates and SDG&E bills that routinely run $300 to $500 a month for moderately-sized homes.

The credit is currently set to stay at 30% through 2032, then step down. There's always political uncertainty around these things, but for now the 30% number is reliable planning material.

What the NEM 3.0 transition really is, stripped of all the policy language, is a shift in who captures the value of solar: from any solar homeowner to solar homeowners who are thoughtful about storage and consumption timing. That's a harder bar to clear than it was two years ago, but it's not an impossible one. The people I've seen make this work are the ones who went in with accurate numbers rather than the numbers an eager salesperson put on a proposal. Get your own numbers.
