Florida gets about 237 sunny days a year. You'd think that makes it the best place in the country to go solar. The truth is more complicated, and the thing that trips up most Florida homeowners has nothing to do with sunshine.

It's the utility companies.

## Why Florida Is a Good-But-Complicated Solar State

Florida ranks 3rd in the country for installed solar capacity, according to SEIA data, with over 12 gigawatts of residential and commercial solar installed as of 2024. The sun is real. The savings potential is real. But Florida's [net metering policy](/net-metering-changes-by-state/) is, politely speaking, a work in progress, and a lot of homeowners find that out after they've already signed a contract.

Here's the short version: Florida eliminated its original 1:1 net metering policy in 2022. The new framework, called "net metering 2.0," means that when your panels produce more electricity than you use and that excess goes back to the grid, you get credited at a lower rate than what you pay for electricity. FPL (Florida Power & Light), which serves about 5.8 million customers, pays avoided-cost rates for surplus power that are substantially below retail. Duke Energy Florida works the same way.

In practical terms, this changes your payback math. A system that might pay itself off in 7 years under full retail net metering could take 9 to 11 years under avoided-cost crediting, depending on how much of your solar production you actually use in real time. The fix, increasingly, is a battery. Self-consumption beats grid export when the export credit is weak.

None of this makes Florida a bad solar state. It makes it a state where system design and usage patterns matter more than they do in, say, New Jersey, which still has much stronger net metering protections. Size your system to your actual consumption, not bigger. Add storage if you can afford it, especially in hurricane country.

The average Florida home uses around 1,200 kWh per month, which is higher than the national average of roughly 900 kWh. Air conditioning is doing a lot of work here. That higher usage actually works in your favor because it means more solar production can be consumed directly rather than exported at the lower avoided-cost rate.

System pricing in Florida has come down meaningfully. EnergySage's market data puts the average installed cost for a residential solar system at around $2.85 per watt before incentives in 2024. For a typical 10 kW system (which is what many Florida homes need), that's roughly $28,500 before incentives.

After the federal Investment Tax Credit, which covers 30% of total system costs and applies through 2032, you're looking at about $19,950 out of pocket for that 10 kW system. Florida also exempts solar equipment from the state sales tax and property tax, which together can save several thousand dollars more. No state income tax credit, though, so the federal ITC is carrying most of the financial weight.

Expected monthly savings vary, but a well-designed system in central or south Florida can realistically offset $150 to $200 per month in electricity costs if you've sized it right and run major loads during peak solar hours. Some homeowners I've spoken with are saving more than that by shifting dishwasher and laundry cycles to midday. Simple, but it adds up.

Payback periods in Florida right now land somewhere between 8 and 12 years for most homeowners, with the shorter end applying to those in FPL territory with efficient homes and the longer end applying to situations with significant shading, high export ratios, or older tile roofs that needed work before installation.

## Batteries: Not Optional Anymore in Hurricane Alley

Florida averages more landfalling hurricanes than any other state. That's not a minor footnote in a solar buying guide; it's a central consideration.

Without a battery, your solar panels go dark during a grid outage. This surprises people. Standard grid-tied systems are required to shut off during outages for lineworker safety. So if you're in South Florida and a Cat 2 rolls through and takes out the grid for four days, your rooftop panels are sitting there doing nothing while you sweat in August.

A battery changes that equation significantly. The Tesla Powerwall 3, currently priced around $12,000 installed, is the most common choice I see in Florida right now. The Enphase IQ Battery 5P is another strong option, particularly if you're pairing it with Enphase microinverters. For larger homes or longer backup needs, some homeowners are stacking two Powerwalls or looking at the Franklin WH5000 as a slightly more affordable alternative.

One thing Florida-specific to keep in mind: the battery incentive situation is improving. If your battery is charged exclusively by solar (not from the grid), it qualifies for the federal 30% ITC alongside your panels. That helps offset the sting of the upfront cost.

The NREL has published research showing that [solar-plus-storage systems](/best-states-for-solar-energy-savings/) in high-irradiance climates like Florida can achieve better financial returns than solar-only when you account for time-of-use rates and avoided demand charges, which some Florida utilities are moving toward for residential customers. The resilience value during hurricane season is harder to put a dollar figure on, but it's real.

## Roof Conditions, HOAs, and the Bureaucratic Reality

Florida roofs take a beating. Before any installer quotes your system, you need an honest assessment of your roof's remaining life. Most solar installations come with a workmanship warranty of 10 to 25 years, but if your concrete tile or barrel tile roof is 15 years old, you're potentially looking at a re-roof mid-system-life, and removing and reinstalling panels for a re-roof runs $1,500 to $4,000 or more depending on system size.

Tile roofs are also more expensive to install on. Some installers charge a premium of $0.20 to $0.40 per watt extra for tile versus asphalt shingle, because the mounting process is more labor-intensive and the risk of tile cracking during installation is real. Get that in writing upfront.

On HOAs: Florida actually has strong legal protections for solar here. Florida Statute 163.04 prohibits HOAs and deed restrictions from banning solar panels outright. They can regulate placement to some extent (they can push for rear-facing if the home has multiple roof aspects), but they cannot deny you the right to install a system. If your HOA tells you they can simply prohibit solar, they're wrong, and a quick letter citing the statute usually resolves it.

Permitting in Florida is generally handled at the county level. Most counties have moved toward streamlined solar permitting, and the state passed legislation in 2021 requiring that residential solar permits be processed within 10 business days. In reality, timelines vary. Miami-Dade tends to run longer than smaller counties. Budget 4 to 8 weeks from contract to installation as a realistic expectation.

## Choosing an Installer Without Getting Burned

Florida has a lot of solar installers. Some are excellent. Some are chasing the volume of a hot market and cutting corners on design, equipment selection, and post-installation support.

A few things I'd insist on before signing anything:

Look at the inverter choice carefully. String inverters are cheaper but perform worse if you have any shading, a complex roof, or multiple orientations. Microinverters (Enphase is the dominant brand) or power optimizers (SolarEdge) give you panel-level monitoring and better performance in real-world Florida roof conditions. For a Florida home that might have one slope shaded by a palm tree or a chimney, this matters more than the salesperson will tell you.

Ask for a production estimate in kWh, not just a dollar figure. The kWh estimate should come from PVWatts or similar modeling software, and you should be able to see the inputs: your address, roof tilt, azimuth, and the system's assumed losses. Any installer who can't or won't show you this work is waving a red flag.

Check their license. Florida requires solar contractors to hold either a state Electrical Contractor license or a Building Contractor license with a solar specialty. You can verify this through the Florida DBPR (Department of Business and Professional Regulation) online lookup in about two minutes. Don't skip this.

Get at least three quotes. EnergySage's marketplace is genuinely useful for this, not because every installer on the platform is perfect, but because it forces competition on price and makes it easy to compare equipment side by side. The average savings from comparing multiple quotes versus taking the first one is around $5,000 to $7,000, according to EnergySage's own data.

The sun in Florida is genuinely exceptional. Nearly 5.5 peak sun hours per day in most of the state, year-round. What separates a good solar investment from a mediocre one here is mostly execution: getting the system sized right, choosing equipment that performs on a real Florida roof with real shading conditions, understanding the net metering rules before you sign anything, and, increasingly, pairing panels with storage so you're actually insulated from grid dependence when the next storm rolls through. The solar math works. You just have to run it correctly.
