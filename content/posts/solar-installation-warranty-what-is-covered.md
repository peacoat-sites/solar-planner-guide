Most homeowners assume their [solar installer](/how-to-choose-a-solar-installer/) "covers everything for 25 years." That assumption has cost people thousands of dollars.

I've watched it happen more than once. A panel stops producing at year eight. The homeowner calls the installer. The installer says the panel is physically fine, so it's a manufacturer issue. The manufacturer says the installer voided coverage by using non-approved mounting hardware. The homeowner is caught in the middle, holding a repair bill for $1,200, with two companies pointing at each other. This scenario plays out constantly, and it happens because most people sign contracts without understanding that a residential [solar system](/how-to-size-a-solar-system-when-the-tax-credit-is-gone/) actually comes with three to four separate warranties, each covering something different, each with its own expiration date and its own fine print.

Here's the number that stopped me cold when I first dug into this: according to the [National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL)](https://www.nrel.gov/), roughly 0.5% of solar panels fail annually in ways that aren't covered by standard workmanship claims. Across 4 million-plus U.S. residential systems, that's potentially tens of thousands of homeowners discovering coverage gaps every single year. And most of them had no idea.

## The Four Warranties (And Why Conflating Them Is a Costly Mistake)

Let me break down exactly what each warranty covers, because this is where the confusion starts.

**Product warranty (equipment warranty):** This covers physical defects in the panel itself. Manufacturing errors, delamination, discoloration from defective materials, junction box failures. The current industry standard from tier-one manufacturers like LG, REC, and Panasonic runs 12 to 25 years. Qcells, one of the more common brands on residential installs right now, offers a 25-year product warranty. SunPower's Maxeon panels offer 40 years, which is genuinely unusual and worth knowing about. What this does NOT cover: cosmetic cracking from a hailstorm, anything caused by improper installation, or output degradation unless it's extreme.

**Performance (power output) warranty:** Separate from the product warranty. This guarantees the panel will still produce a specified percentage of its rated wattage after a set number of years. Most manufacturers guarantee 80-85% output at year 25. The degradation rate ceiling is typically 0.5-0.7% per year. What this does NOT cover: system-level underperformance caused by shading, inverter issues, or poor string design. The manufacturer only warrants the individual panel.

**Inverter warranty:** String inverters (brands like SMA, Fronius, SolarEdge) typically come with 10-year standard warranties, with optional extensions to 20 years for an additional cost usually ranging $150-$400. Microinverters from Enphase come with a 25-year warranty standard. This gap matters more than most people realize, string inverters have a meaningful failure rate around years 10-15, and replacing one currently runs $1,000-$3,000 installed depending on system size.

**Workmanship/installation warranty:** This is the one that varies most wildly and the one your installer controls entirely. It covers roof penetration leaks, wiring problems, improper grounding, mechanical failures caused by bad mounting. I've seen this range from a single year (a few cut-rate installers) to a lifetime warranty (SunPower's dealer network). The national average from EnergySage's installer data sits around 10 years, but that number masks huge variation.

I'll be honest: the first time I read through a standard residential solar contract, I thought I understood it. Then a reader from Sacramento, Melissa, emailed me describing a situation where her monitoring system showed a 23% production shortfall. Her panels were physically undamaged. Her installer said, "performance is within warranty specs on each individual panel." Technically true. Systemically, her roof was generating significantly less than the production estimate she'd been sold.

That's a real gap. The performance warranty covers individual panels, not the system design. If your installer undersized the array, pointed panels suboptimally, or underestimated shading from a neighbor's tree that was already there at install time, that's not typically covered by any warranty. It might be addressed through a production guarantee (a separate contractual promise about annual kWh output, sometimes offered but not universal), but warranties and production guarantees are different things.

Other common exclusions I've seen buried in the fine print:

- Acts of God (hail, hurricane wind damage) -- usually the domain of homeowner's insurance, not solar warranties
- Damage from "improper maintenance" (this one's vague enough to be dangerous)
- Cosmetic damage that doesn't affect output
- Panels installed on non-permitted structures
- Performance impacts from soiling or shading that wasn't present at installation

**What surprised me** the most digging through manufacturer documentation was the soiling exclusion. In dry climates like Phoenix or Albuquerque, panel soiling can reduce output 5-10% seasonally. None of that is covered by any warranty. It's a maintenance issue, full stop.

## When Installers Go Out of Business

This is the uncomfortable topic that [SEIA](https://www.seia.org/) has flagged in recent years: installer business failure rates in solar are not trivial. The industry saw meaningful consolidation, especially among smaller regional installers.

Here's how it typically works: if your installer closes, your panel and inverter manufacturer warranties remain valid, you'd contact the manufacturer directly. But your workmanship warranty is essentially gone. That roof penetration that starts leaking at year 9? Nobody's obligated to fix it.

Worked example: Tampa homeowner, 8.4 kW system installed in 2021 by a regional installer that closed in late 2023. Roof flashing failure discovered in 2025. Panel manufacturer (REC) confirmed panels were fine. Installer was defunct. Homeowner paid $2,400 out of pocket for reroofing around penetrations. Had the homeowner chosen an installer with a third-party warranty backstop, the outcome would have been different.

Some larger installers (Sunrun, Palmetto, Sunnova) maintain warranty obligations through their own balance sheets or third-party insurers. Worth asking about specifically.

## What to Actually Ask Before You Sign

I'm not going to pretend there's a single document that makes this simple. But in my experience reviewing dozens of solar contracts, there are five questions that separate a solid warranty package from a liability:

1. Who backs the workmanship warranty if the installer closes? (Ask for proof of insurance or bonding.)
2. Is the performance warranty per-panel or system-level? (Per-panel is standard; system-level is better but rare outside of bundled offers.)
3. What's the inverter warranty, and what does extending it cost? (For a string inverter, the extension is almost always worth it.)
4. What specific events or conditions would void my equipment warranty? (Get this in writing. "Improper maintenance" needs to be defined.)
5. Does the manufacturer have a U.S.-based claims process, or do I have to ship panels internationally? (Some lesser-known brands get murky here fast.)

Worked example: A reader in Austin compared two bids. Both were similar in price ($28,400 vs. $29,100). Installer A offered a 10-year workmanship warranty with no backstop. Installer B offered 25 years, bonded, through a company that had been operating 15+ years. The $700 difference was, in my view, almost irrelevant next to that gap in coverage. He went with Installer B.

Catching a 15% production drop at month 3 versus year 7 makes a significant difference in your ability to make a warranty claim while documentation is still fresh.
